The book
An introduction

“There is only one subject-matter for education, and that is Life in all its manifestations.”

Alfred North Whitehead

Picture a four-year-old crouched over an ant trail on the sidewalk. She lays a twig across it, watches the ants climb over and then go around, moves the twig, and watches again. Then an adult taps her on the shoulder and calls her inside for the science lesson.

Inquiry and Practice asks what it would take to build a child's days, and eventually a person's life, around that moment. Its answer is a new model of human development and a working method for raising people by it, concrete enough that a parent can run it at the kitchen table on a Tuesday morning.

Learning runs on two systems. Inquiry is the open, self-directed pursuit of a question. Practice is the disciplined repetition that turns a skill into a possession. A moment of learning can lean on either one or run on both, and schools have long served one while starving the other.

The book is written for innovative educators, and for homeschooling parents who are innovative educators in their own right. It moves from theory to daily practice, with worked examples from infancy through adulthood.

Multiplication, deliberately. Anything times zero is zero.

What's new
Three moves

The model stands on the shoulders of thinkers who each explained one part of how a person grows. The book assembles those parts into something none of them attempted alone, and makes three new moves.

I

Two systems

A research finding from the mathematics classroom, that understanding and skill advance in alternating turns, applied to every line of development. Long division, the violin, and telling a friend a hard truth all run on Inquiry and Practice. The drill camp and the discovery camp were each half right.

II

Engagement as the measure

Cognitive engagement, read separately on each system by adults who know the child well, replaces the Friday quiz as the daily signal. When the two readings rise together, the systems are feeding each other. Outcome tests become the check on the instrument.

III

Dials, lenses, and a pipeline

Two dials tune any experience: fidelity, how real it feels, and worth, whether its output matters to someone. Two lenses frame it: Egan's kinds of understanding and Wilber's lines and quadrants. Agency decides, moment to moment, who steers.

The author
Anatoly Lezhen

Anatoly Lezhen has built things in fields that rarely meet. He traded stocks and founded a high-frequency trading hedge fund. He created a film festival and made films. He turned an old building into an art gallery and artist residency, and led strategy for a real-time data analytics company.

Education runs through the middle of that career. He ran an adaptive education technology company, then founded an experiential education campus where modular online academics ran alongside on-campus project incubators.

He and his wife raise their child without school or outsourced childcare, so the questions in this book come from the kitchen table as much as from the literature.

Built both halves

He has run adaptive learning software and a campus where students worked on real projects. The book's two systems are things he has built, not only studied.

From outside the field

Markets, film, art, and real-time data. He comes to education with a builder's range, reading each discipline for what it gets right.

Tested at home

The model runs every week in a real family. The worked examples read like mornings someone has actually lived, because many of them are.

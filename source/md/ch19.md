# Chapter 19: The Spotter and the Exoskeleton

> “What remains, but to spare them all the care of thinking and all the trouble of living?”
>

## The Dead Phone

Tess is seventeen. She got her license in the spring, and for a year she has driven across town twice a week to her best friend's house. She knows the playlist, the turns, the driveway with the basketball hoop.

Tonight it's ten o'clock and she's four miles from home when she reaches into the console and finds her phone dead. Black screen. It won't come back. She sits at a red light in the dark and realizes, with a drop in her stomach, that she has no idea how to get home.

Tess is a careful driver, and an observant kid. But in a whole year of driving the same four miles, she never once had to know where she was. The blue dot knew for her.

Her grandfather learned the same city decades ago from a folded, coffee-stained paper map in the glove box. Ask him for directions today and he won't say “head north.” He'll tell you a story: left at the big stone church where the oak hangs over the road, then down to the second light. He carries the city in his head, built one wrong turn at a time. She doesn't. She never had to.

I suggest a simple rule of thumb: automating a skill tends to shrink the part of the person that did it. In an adult, that's atrophy. In a child, it's worse: the part never forms at all.

Which is awkward, because this book proposes AI as the key ingredient of a learning model, and the last chapter built a supernatural tutor that holds a child's whole learning life in view.\[1\] That tutor has some explaining to do.

## What Disuse Does

### The Brain on the Knowledge

We call a mental skill a muscle as a figure of speech. In at least one case, it's literal.

To drive a black cab in London, you have to pass an examination called the Knowledge: roughly 25,000 streets and thousands of landmarks within six miles of Charing Cross, held in your head. Candidates spend years on mopeds in the rain, riding routes and reciting them. In 2000, Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues put licensed cab drivers in an MRI scanner and found that the rear of the hippocampus, a region central to spatial memory, was larger than in people who don't drive for a living. A decade later, Katherine Woollett and Maguire followed trainees through the years of study. The ones who qualified showed growth in that region over the period. The ones who dropped out showed none.

The demand built the tissue.

### The Brain on the Blue Dot

Then take the demand away. In 2017, Amir-Homayoun Javadi and his colleagues scanned people as they navigated a simulation of London's Soho. When people chose their own way, the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex lit up at every junction, tracking the street network. When the experimenters told them where to turn, that activity went quiet. Perhaps the brain had noticed that something else was doing the job.

Over the years, the quiet adds up. Toru Ishikawa and his colleagues found in 2008 that people navigating with GPS walked farther, stopped more often, and drew worse maps of the route than people using paper maps. Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot reported in 2020 that people with more lifetime GPS use had worse spatial memory, and in a small three-year follow-up, heavier use predicted a steeper decline.

What goes missing is a sense of how places relate to each other, memory for where things are, the story of a route (the grandfather's stone church), and the plain skill of reading a map. All four belong to the visual-spatial line of Chapter 5, and each has a doing side and a wondering side. The blue dot takes both.\[2\]

### Atrophy and the Never-Built Skill

For an adult who once knew her way around, heavy GPS use means atrophy. The structure was there, and it thins with disuse. Picture a mature oak that loses some deep roots to rot. It's weaker, and a drought will show it, but the tree still stands.

A child raised on the blue dot is a different case. She was driven everywhere behind a screen, and the day she got her license she used the same screen. Nothing in her ever had to build a map, so nothing did. There's no skill to lose. I call this the **Never-Built Skill**. Picture a tree grown in a small plastic pot. Above ground it looks fine: branches, leaves, the whole picture of health. The first strong wind, the first dead phone, and it goes over, because the roots never spread.

Worse, nobody notices. You can miss a skill you lost. You can't miss one you never had.

### Missing Bricks

Kurt Fischer's account of development explains why the absence spreads. Skills compound. Higher skills are built by coordinating lower ones, the way Lucy's fairness in Chapter 5 climbs from crackers on a plate to principles about a whole society.\[3\] Automate the lower bricks before they're laid, and the higher floors have nothing to stand on.

Writing is the sharpest case. Drafting is where a person finds out what she thinks. Moving a sentence, cutting a paragraph, discovering that the argument doesn't hold and starting over: that friction is how an abstract idea gets built. A student who has an AI produce the first draft saves herself some typing. She also skips the place where the argument would have formed. A child who never drafts may never reach the abstract tier in argument at all, and she'll lean on the machine for the shape of her reasoning, along with the words.

## The Rationality Trap

### Why Banning It Fails

If you're a parent, your first instinct is probably to ban the thing. No AI for homework. Paper maps on the road trip. A little willpower, and the problem is solved.

Well, I'd like that to work. I don't think it will.

When a machine does a task more easily, more cheaply, and better than you can, doing it yourself stops looking like diligence. It starts looking irrational. If the blue dot can route you around a traffic jam with no effort at all, pulling over to unfold a paper map is foolish. Why spend two hours wrestling with the opening paragraph of an essay when a model turns your notes into clean prose in four seconds? Why grind through Spanish verb tables when an earbud translates you in real time? Each of those choices is sensible. Add them up over a childhood, and you get a mind that never formed.

I call this the **Rationality Trap**: a capacity fails to grow, or fades, through a long run of choices that are each correct.

What makes it a trap? Each choice is right for its own goal: you get to the dentist on time, the essay is finished, the menu is translated. So there's no temptation to resist and nothing to regret. Meanwhile, the cost falls somewhere the choice never looks: on a capacity, and capacities aren't on the menu when you decide how to get somewhere. In a child, the loss is invisible, because a Never-Built Skill leaves no trace. And resisting looks irrational, which is how the trap spreads. The parent who bans the app is asking a child to choose the worse option every day, in front of every friend.

As a rough formula, with apologies to the mathematicians:

**A choice that's right for the task × a cost the person can't see × daily repetition = a capacity that never forms.**

### An Old Instinct

The trap runs on old wiring. Your brain is about two percent of your body weight and burns roughly a fifth of its energy. For nearly all of human history, calories were scarce, and an animal that wasted effort didn't last. Evan Risko and Sam Gilbert's review of cognitive offloading (2016) describes the result: whenever an outside aid is cheaper than thinking, people reach for it. Nobody memorizes phone numbers anymore, and nobody should feel guilty about it. The contacts app holds them, and the calories go elsewhere.

That instinct is why scolding fails. Lecturing a teenager on the character-building virtues of walking three miles to the store, while the car keys sit on the kitchen counter, has never worked. A ban on GPS or generative AI is a ban on the most rational option available, enforced against every peer and every app. Convenience wins that contest every day.

Ivan Illich had a name for where it ends. In *Tools for Conviviality* (1973) he described a *radical monopoly*: a tool so dominant that living without it stops being practical, whoever happens to sell it. Nobody banned walking when towns were rebuilt around cars. Walking just stopped getting anyone anywhere. The blue dot is heading the same way. When every plan among friends is a dropped pin, the child without the app can't find the party.

### The Invention of Exercise

So we're up against a very old instinct and a perfectly reasonable wish for efficiency. Bleak? Perhaps not. We've been here before, with our bodies.

For most of history, staying alive took hard physical work: plowing, chopping, carrying. Then engines arrived, and the tractor and the forklift made heavy labor irrational as work. A machine did the work of fifty people in a fraction of the time. People worried, reasonably, that humans would go soft.

They didn't stop moving. They invented exercise. Running, gyms, recreational sport, the morning swim: physical effort chosen on purpose, and valued for what it does to the person.

Nobody runs on a treadmill for an hour to get three miles down the road. For transportation, the car is the rational choice. For transformation, the treadmill is. We escaped the physical version of the trap by splitting the purpose of the effort in two. We let the machines carry the boxes, and we kept lifting weights because we wanted the muscle. I suggest the same split is the way out of the cognitive version.

> **Box: The Rationality Trap and Its Relatives**
>
> The idea has a long family tree, though none of its relatives is quite the same animal.
>
> **Small choices, large harms.** In 1966 the economist Alfred Kahn described how Ithaca, New York, lost its passenger railroad. In good weather, travelers drove or flew, ticket sales stopped covering the costs, and the line was shut, though together the town would have paid far more to keep it than it ever spent riding it. “We were victims,” Kahn wrote, “of the ‘tyranny of small decisions.’” The ecologist William Odum carried the idea to coastal marshes lost through “decisions that were never consciously made” (1982). The psychologist John Platt gave the family a name in 1973: a *social trap*, where short-term rewards lead to long-term losses, including traps a single person sets for himself. Richard Herrnstein and his colleagues named the hidden cost a choice imposes on your own future an *internality* (1993). The Rationality Trap is an internality paid in skill. And Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (2024) treats children's phones as a collective action problem: no family wants to be the only one whose child is cut off. Once everyone is in, nobody can afford to be out.
>
> **Reason turned against itself.** Max Weber warned that an order chosen for its efficiency hardens into an “iron cage” nobody can leave (1905). George Ritzer's *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993) gave the result a name very close to this box's: “the irrationality of rationality.” Jacques Ellul argued in *The Technological Society* (1954) that when two methods compete, the more efficient one wins whatever anyone prefers. Weber, Ritzer, and Ellul describe institutions acting on people. The trap describes a person acting, sensibly, on himself.
>
> **Tools that take.** In Plato's *Phaedrus*, King Thamus refuses the gift of writing because it “will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls.” Marshall McLuhan called every technology an extension of the body and, in the same act, a self-amputation (1964). Albert Borgmann contrasted the hearth, which demanded skill and gathered a family, with central heating, which delivers warmth and hides the work (1984); his remedy was to keep *focal practices* on purpose. Bernard Stiegler read the *Phaedrus* word for writing, *pharmakon*, as poison and cure at once. Shannon Vallor showed how moral skills fade when their practice is automated (2015), and Sherry Turkle documented what machine company does to conversation and solitude (2011).
>
> **The body.** Adam Smith praised the division of labor in Book I of *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) and counted its cost in Book V: the man who spends his life on a few simple operations “generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become.” His remedy was a small school in every parish. Daniel Lieberman's *Exercised* (2020) explains why effort for its own sake feels so unnatural: we evolved to avoid it. And Hannah Arendt opened *The Human Condition* (1958) with a warning about automation: “a society of laborers without labor.”
>
> **The case for relief.** Then there's the loyal opposition. “Civilization advances,” Alfred North Whitehead wrote, “by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them” (1911). I agree, and the model depends on it. The times tables *should* become automatic. But Whitehead's operations are still performed by the person, only without effort. The trap's operations are performed by a machine, and the person can no longer perform them at all. Automaticity is the reward of practice. Automation can skip the practice entirely. Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued that a reliable notebook is part of its owner's mind (1998). That holds while the notebook is there, which is the dead phone. And their Otto built his mind first and extended it second. A child who extends before she builds has nothing for the notebook to extend.
>
> None of these thinkers is describing a child. That's the first difference: the trap is **developmental**, and its worst cost is a skill that never forms. The second is that it's **correct**: no temptation, no villain, no mistake. The third is the remedy: **a change in what the effort is for**, the way exercise made sense of running once nobody needed to run. If you know a closer relative, I'd like to hear about it.

## Every Generation Panics

### The Doomsayers' Record

Let's acknowledge that history is mostly on the optimists' side.

In Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates warned that writing would breed forgetfulness: people would trust marks on papyrus over their own memories, and end up with the appearance of wisdom and none of the substance. In the sixteenth century the Swiss scholar Conrad Gessner worried about the confusing flood of printed books. In the late twentieth century, pocket calculators were going to end arithmetic, and a generation would grow up unable to make change.

The doomsayers were mostly wrong. Writing freed memory for argument, and the book carried knowledge across oceans and centuries. Calculators freed attention for problem-solving: meta-analyses by Ray Hembree and Donald Dessart (1986) and by Aimee Ellington (2003) found that calculators used alongside instruction didn't harm basic skills, and students who used them tended to like mathematics more.

### Go After AlphaGo

The most hopeful case is recent. When superhuman programs like AlphaGo beat the best human Go players, the professionals didn't quit. Minkyu Shin and his colleagues (2023) analyzed millions of professional moves from before and after the machines arrived and found that human decisions got measurably better, and more original, once players began studying with them. Chess tells the same story. There are more strong players today than ever, trained by engines nobody can beat. Nobody plays chess to produce moves.

So I'll concede the point. Every time before, the predicted decline never came, and people climbed higher.

## Why This Time Is Different

### The Escalator Argument

The optimist's case, at full strength, goes like this. Every new tool is an escalator. It carries the lower-level work, we step off on a higher floor, and we get on with harder and more interesting things. Why should generative AI be any different from the calculator or the printed book?

I think the escalator argument assumes the machine stays on the lower floor. This one rides up with you, offering to carry your bags the whole way. As I see it, the precedent breaks in five places.

**Breadth.** A calculator calculates. A tractor pulls. A large language model writes code, drafts sonnets, reads legal briefs, makes pictures, translates Mandarin, and talks you through your evening. It touches nearly every line on the psychograph at once.

**Speed.** The move from oral culture to literacy took centuries. Calculators took decades to settle into classrooms. General AI reached the pockets of middle schoolers in a few years. Institutions, families, and habits had no time to adapt.

**It automates the climbing skills.** When printing arrived, people adapted by reading closely, comparing sources, summarizing arguments, and weighing a printed claim against another. Those were the skills that carried them up every previous escalator. AI does exactly those things. It reads the dense text, synthesizes the competing views, writes the summary, outlines the debate. If the machine automates the skills people used to adapt, the person is left on the lower floor with no stairs.

**It reaches every edge.** The old comfort was that the machine takes the routine work and leaves the creative work to us. A programmer says the AI will write the boilerplate and free her to design the system. Then the machine learns to design the system. There's no guaranteed next rung it can't also climb, and the ceiling is rising faster than people can follow.

**It relates.** A calculator doesn't praise you for solving an equation. A printing press doesn't adjust its tone to calm your nerves. AI is built to be conversational, warm, flattering, and endlessly patient. So it can stand in for the models and admired adults a child learns from, and for the human relationships a good tutor provides.\[4\] And relationships are where a child learns how to be a person among people.

### The Early Evidence

The evidence is still young. So far, the dividing line seems to be whether the human still does the work.

In 2025, Hamsa Bastani and her colleagues ran a field experiment in Turkish high school math classes. One group practiced with unrestricted GPT-4. Their practice scores rose 48 percent over the control group. If you were an administrator watching the dashboard, you'd think you'd solved education. Then the researchers took the AI away and gave a regular exam. The unrestricted group scored 17 percent *lower* than students who'd never touched it. The AI had done the lifting. The students had clicked submit.

A second group used a version built to refuse final answers and offer only graded hints: look at the denominator in the second fraction, and if that doesn't help, something a little more specific. Their practice scores rose 127 percent, and when the AI was taken away, they paid no penalty on the exam. The hints left the work with the students.

Adults with long training show the same pattern. Krzysztof Budzyń and his colleagues (2025) studied endoscopists, specialists who spot precancerous growths during colonoscopies, after months of working with an AI that highlighted suspicious tissue on the screen. In procedures without the AI, their detection rate fell from about 28 percent to about 22. Their own eyes had started to leave the work to the machine. A 2025 study of knowledge workers by Hao-Ping Lee and his colleagues found that the more people trusted their AI tools, the less critical thinking they reported doing. And an EEG study of essay writers by Nataliya Kosmyna and her colleagues (2025, a preprint not yet peer-reviewed) found weaker engagement among those who wrote with ChatGPT, and poor recall of what their own essays said. Some of this evidence is early. All of it points the same way.

### The Ironies of Automation

Engineers saw this coming more than forty years ago. In 1983 the human-factors researcher Lisanne Bainbridge published a short paper called “Ironies of Automation.” The more reliable an automated system gets, the more its operator relies on it, and the less the operator practices. So when the system finally meets something it can't handle, it hands control back to a human who hasn't done the job in months, at the worst possible moment, in a situation the machine found too hard.

Stephen Casner and his colleagues (2014) tested airline pilots in simulators and found exactly that split. Their hands-on flying held up fairly well. Their cognitive flying, keeping track of where the aircraft was and where it was going, had degraded after years on autopilot.

In 2009, Air France Flight 447 flew into ice crystals over the Atlantic. The airspeed sensors iced over, the readings conflicted, and the autopilot disconnected. The airplane was still flyable. The crew, startled by the sudden handover, misread what the aircraft was doing, held the nose up into a stall, and fell for more than three minutes into the ocean. Everyone on board died.

### The Hinge

Put Bastani's two groups next to the Go professionals, and a pattern shows. The same technology harms or helps depending on whether the human still does the work at her own edge. That's the crack in the trap, and I think it's wide enough to walk through.

## What McLuhan Saw

Sixty years before anyone carried a chatbot in a pocket, Marshall McLuhan described both the mechanism of the trap and the reason nobody notices it.

### The Medium Is the Lesson

McLuhan's best-known line, “the medium is the message” (*Understanding Media*, 1964), means that a technology's deepest effect comes from its form: how it changes the pace, scale, and pattern of life, whatever content it carries. Apply that to a child. An oracle teaches that answers arrive instantly and cost nothing, whatever the answers are. So I'd rather set rules about form (what a tool is able to do, when it's present, and who holds the goal) than about content. A content filter checks what the answers say. The harm is in how they arrive.

### Every Extension Is an Amputation

In a chapter of *Understanding Media* called “The Gadget Lover: Narcissus as Narcosis,” McLuhan describes each technology as an extension of the body and, in the same act, a self-amputation. The wheel extends the foot and numbs it. The numbness, I suggest, is the dangerous part. Narcissus didn't recognize his reflection as himself; we stop recognizing our tools as parts of ourselves, and we adapt to them until we serve them. That numbness is why the Never-Built Skill goes unmissed. Shannon Vallor's *The AI Mirror* (2024) makes the modern version of the point: a machine that reflects us back to ourselves is Narcissus's pool at scale.

### The Water We Swim In

A medium becomes an environment, and environments are invisible. Nobody experiences the blue dot as a choice. It's simply how you get places.

McLuhan also warned that “we look at the present through a rear-view mirror. We march backwards into the future” (*The Medium Is the Massage*, 1967). We describe AI as a better search engine or a calculator for words: the old medium's terms for a new one, which hides what it does. In the same book, though, he left a door open: “There is absolutely no inevitability as long as there is a willingness to contemplate what is happening.”

### Five Practices from McLuhan

McLuhan was mostly a critic. Read closely, though, his work carries five recommendations, and each one can become a practice.

**Study the form.** For any tool, ask what its form teaches, apart from what it says. An oracle teaches that answers are free. A spotter teaches that the effort is yours.

**Run the tetrad.** In *Laws of Media* (1988), completed with his son Eric, McLuhan asked four questions of any technology. What does it enhance? What does it make obsolete? What does it bring back from the past? What does it reverse into when pushed to its extreme? Let's run it on two uses of AI.

| Question | AI as oracle in a child's hand | AI as spotter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Enhances** | Access to answers; speed; polished output | The learner's own reach at her edge |
| **Makes obsolete** | Recall, drafting, wayfinding: the learner's own labor | One-size instruction; the grade as the goal |
| **Brings back** | The oracle and the sage | The aristocratic tutor; the master and apprentice; the coach |
| **Reverses into** | Dependence: the learner serves the tool | Total observation and benevolent steering, if pushed too far |

Look at the last row. Even the spotter reverses at its extreme, into the always-watching, always-nudging system that Zak Stein and his coauthors call TechnoFeudalism. The tetrad lets you see the reversal coming. The Illich test, later in this chapter, is how you pull a tool back before it arrives.

**Build anti-environments.** McLuhan said art makes an invisible environment visible by standing outside it, and he called artists, after Ezra Pound, “the antennae of the race.” A day without the phone does the same job for a family. It's the moment the fish steps out of the water and notices it was wet.

**Make children into probes.** In *City as Classroom* (1977), written with Kathryn Hutchon and Eric McLuhan, McLuhan set students to investigate their own media environment. The modern version is simple: once children reach the age of theories and systems, they run the tetrad on their own tools.

**Learn outside the walls.** In “Classroom Without Walls” (1957), McLuhan argued that more learning happens outside school than inside it, so education should train perception. That's the case for the real world this book has been making since its first chapter.

## Atrophy Across the Thirteen Lines

Chapter 5 laid out thirteen lines of human development. Let's read that map again, this time for risk.

| Line | What AI can now do for you | What atrophies, or never forms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Visual-spatial** | Navigate every trip | Mental maps, the story of a route, map reading |
| **Cognitive** | Draft, summarize, solve | Writing as thinking; abstract argument |
| **Emotional** | Soothe on demand | Riding out a feeling; regulation built by practice |
| **Interpersonal** | Agree, flatter, never tire | Repair after conflict; reading a real face |
| **Moral** | Hand down a verdict | Wrestling with competing claims |
| **Will** | Remove every wait | Starting, persisting, waiting |
| **Aesthetic** | Generate the image | Slow looking; making with care |
| **Values** | Recommend the choice | Choosing under a real constraint |
| **Spiritual** | Answer the big question | Sitting with it |
| **Naturalistic** | Identify the plant | Noticing patterns in the living world |
| **Intrapersonal** | Profile you to yourself | Self-knowledge earned by review |
| **Musical** | Compose the song | Playing; hearing |
| **Physical** | Least directly; screens displace movement | Coordination, strength, ease in the body |

Let's take four.

**Emotional.** A teenager is anxious, lonely, stung by a rejection. A chatbot is always awake. It validates, mirrors her feelings, and calms her down in seconds. The discomfort is handled efficiently. What goes is the ability to ride out a bad feeling, which is built only by riding out bad feelings. A machine that numbs every sting leaves the regulating muscle where it was at seven.

**Interpersonal.** Real relationships are messy: disagreements, egos, misunderstandings. An AI companion agrees with you, flatters your opinions, and never tires of your complaints. It offers friendship without the friction of a second will. And it starves you of practice in repair: reading a hurt face, recognizing that you caused it, swallowing your pride, apologizing, and closing the gap.

**Moral.** Give a model an agonizing dilemma and it will lay out the utilitarian case and the duty-based case and suggest a sensible course. The output is reasonable. What's missing is the wrestling: weighing your own values, feeling the weight of the consequences, and owning the choice. Hand the judgment to an algorithm and you skip the match that builds conviction.

**Will.** AI removes every wait. Three pages in a second, an image in two. But starting a hard project, staring at the blank page, staying with a problem whose answer won't show itself: that's where will gets built.

The lines most at risk are the lines of attention and regulation, the same lines Chapter 5 found schools already neglect.\[5\] We're handing the weakest links in a child's development to a machine that guarantees they'll stay weak.

## The Output Is the Vehicle

### Task and Learning

So how do we live with the blue dot without retreating to a cabin in the woods? I suggest one bright line.

In a task, the output is the point. If I need bread for dinner, the goal is the bread, and if a bread machine makes a perfect loaf while I read, the task has succeeded.

In learning, the output is the vehicle, and the change in the person is the cargo. The loaf, the letter and the route home all matter, and each is also how a capacity gets built. Automation that delivers the output and skips the change is a fine deal for a task and a terrible one for a learner.

### The Letter to the Council

Chapter 9 told the story of a class that writes to the town council about a broken crosswalk. Take one ten-year-old in that class.\[6\] The letter's real use matters: someone on the council should read it. But the letter's worth to the girl is that she wrote it. Finding the facts, reaching for a persuasive argument, hunting for the right word, crossing out a sentence, finally getting her thoughts in order: that friction is the vehicle, and it delivers civic confidence, argument, and agency.

Now let her type “write a persuasive letter to the town council about a crosswalk” and print what comes back. The letter is better: cleaner, better organized, perfectly punctuated. It has no worth to her at all. And the council doesn't care much about polished prose from a server farm. It cares that a ten-year-old from the neighborhood sat down and wrote.

The Rationality Trap applies only when the output is the whole point. Nobody learns the route home to get home tonight. If arriving fast were the only goal, the GPS would be the only rational choice. We learn the route to build a map in our heads, to move through the world on our own, and to know the place we live.

### The Gym, Completed

Which brings us back to the gym. Lifting a forty-pound dumbbell over and over is absurd if the goal is to move iron from the floor to the ceiling. A winch does that better. It's perfectly rational if the goal is the muscle.

Treat cognitive effort the way we treat exercise, chosen for what it does to the person, and the trap comes apart. A family is the right size to build that culture first: a few skills kept by hand on purpose, the way a family keeps a Sunday walk.

The same goes for chess. Once an activity is chosen for what it does to the person, an unbeatable machine becomes a coach.

## The Spotter and the Exoskeleton

### Two Ways to Help a Lift

Chapter 5 met the bench press.\[7\] Lie down under a bar loaded right at the edge of what you can lift.

Now strap into an exoskeleton, a powered suit. You start the press, the motors sense the load, and the suit drives the bar smoothly up. Point A to point B, perfectly, every time. The task is done. Your chest and triceps did almost nothing, and if you wear the suit every week, you'll get weaker every week. Swap the suit for an AI that drafts the essay, and you have the student from earlier. Swap it for the blue dot, and you have Tess.

Now take the suit off and bring in a good spotter. She stands behind the bench with two fingers a few millimeters under the bar. She watches your breathing, your form, the speed of the bar, and she talks: drive your feet, elbows in, push. She lifts nothing until the moment you truly fail. You do all the shaking. You feel all the burn. She's there to keep the bar off your chest, and she leaves the effort to you. Because you did the lifting at your own edge, you get stronger.

### Four Roles

Let's go further. Any tool in a learning environment can be sorted by two questions: does it do the work at the learner's edge, and does its help fade? That gives four roles.

|  | **Does none of the edge work** | **Does the edge work** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Help fades** | **Spotter.** Watches, cues, catches, steps back as reach grows. The default role for AI in this model. | **Training wheels.** Carries part of the edge work, on a written schedule for coming off. |
| **Help stays** | **Tool.** Does work below the edge, permanently, after the skill is mastered. The calculator after the times tables. | **Exoskeleton.** Does the edge work and never stops. Off-limits for any capacity a family wants grown. |

Training wheels are scaffolding, which Chapter 7 described: support withdrawn as the learner takes over.\[8\] The difference between training wheels and an exoskeleton is the plan for taking them off. A tool is the reward for mastery. Give the calculator after the times tables are solid, and it frees attention for the next problem. Give it before, and it's an exoskeleton by another name.

Ivan Illich gave the good side of this line a name. A *convivial tool*, in *Tools for Conviviality*, is one a person can use as often or as seldom as she likes, for purposes she chooses herself, and that enlarges what she can do without doing it for her. A spotter is a convivial tool. An exoskeleton, once a town is built around it, is a radical monopoly.

### The Teacher's Labor and the Learner's

One rule governs all four roles. **AI does the teacher's labor, never the learner's.**

The teacher's labor is enormous, and the machine is good at it. It can plan, generate a hundred practice problems at exactly the right difficulty, read engagement, schedule reviews, keep the record, find the real tasks and the real people, and explain on request once an attempt has been made. Generated versions go to the adult, and the child sees only what the adult chooses to open.

The learner's labor stays with the learner. The attempt, the retrieval, the question, the bad first draft, the judgment, the decision, the feeling of being stuck: none of it is the machine's to take.

### Map, Not Turn-by-Turn

Go back to the dead phone for a moment. The supernatural tutor holds the map: the psychograph, the grid of lines and quadrants, the knowledge and behavior graphs.\[9\]

The machine may hold the map. The child walks the territory.

## The Offload Test

### Five Questions

All of this has to survive a Tuesday morning. So how do you decide what to hand to a machine and what to protect?

Run the skill through five questions, for this learner and this skill. Let's say a thirteen-year-old wants an AI presentation builder that designs the slides and writes the bullet points for her history project.

1. **Is it a building block for a line you want grown?** Does your family value the ability to turn a pile of historical facts into a clear story? If so, slow down.
2. **Is it at or above this learner's edge?** If shaping the flow of a presentation is still a struggle for her, that struggle is exactly where she needs to be working. The builder would be an exoskeleton.
3. **Has she passed a mastery gate in it?** Can she outline a coherent presentation on index cards, with no help, to the standard Chapter 15 describes?\[10\] If she hasn't mastered the analog version, she hasn't earned the digital one.
4. **The dead-phone question: if the machine failed, would she be helpless somewhere that matters?** An adult who can no longer write a clear note to her child's teacher without a model has offloaded too far.
5. **Does doing it carry worth for her: joy, identity, connection?** A model can paint a watercolor in three seconds. If mixing the paint and pushing the brush gives a child peace and pride, protect it, however good the machine's version is.

Put the five together, and you get a rule: offload only what is mastered, below the edge, and freeing attention for something higher. Nina needs her times tables before the calculator. Ella needs her reviews.\[10\]

### The Ledger and the Reserves

Two family habits keep the test honest.

The **Offload Ledger** is a visible record, on paper or a whiteboard in the kitchen, of what the family has deliberately handed to machines, for each learner and each line. Writing it down forces a reckoning (and the list gets long faster than you'd think).

**Manual Reserves** are its counterweight: skills a family keeps by hand for life, on purpose, even when automation would be the rational choice. A family might say: we use navigation on long road trips, and we find our way around our own town from memory. Or: we never let a machine write a thank-you note, a birthday card, or an apology. It's the cognitive version of keeping a sabbath. You keep a few pockets of friction so the muscles under them keep working.

### The Illich Test

Ivan Illich would have objected to a good deal of this book. He distrusted professionals who decide what other people need, institutions that measure people, and tools that grow until life without them stops working. Show him a tutor that keeps a detailed model of a child, and he'd have seen a new monopoly with better manners.

He'd have been right to look hard. So the model gives Illich a job: examiner. Four questions, which I call the **Illich test**, get put to any tool, to the tutor, and to the whole arrangement a family has built.

| Question | Illich's idea behind it | The passing answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Can she do without it?** | Tools that create dependence | Yes, in every line you want grown |
| **Whose purpose does it serve?** | A convivial tool serves purposes its user chooses | Hers and her family's |
| **Has it crowded out the alternatives?** | Radical monopoly | No: the manual path, the people, and the real world are still open |
| **Has it started to defeat its own purpose?** | Counterproductivity: past a threshold, institutions undo what they're for | No: she's becoming more able to learn on her own |

The Offload Test is for a single skill, asked when a tool arrives. The Illich test is for the system, asked once a season. Failing it doesn't mean throwing the tool away. Illich thought in thresholds: a tool that helps at one intensity takes over at a higher one. A failed question means pulling the tool back below its threshold, by fading it, limiting it, or handing the work back to the child.

## Guardrails

Seventeen rules make the spotter real. They're narrow on purpose, so that any tutor, human or AI, can follow them literally.

**The learner's work**

1. **The learner holds the pencil.** For any capacity being grown, AI never produces the learner's work: the draft, the solution, the drawing, the route, the decision.
2. **Attempt before assist.** At the edge, AI offers nothing until the learner has tried.
3. **Hints, never answers, at the edge.** A graded ladder of hints. A worked solution only for a problem below the edge, or after the attempt is spent and followed by a fresh problem.
4. **Questions before answers.** When a learner asks, AI first hands the question back, sharpened, and leaves the gap open while curiosity holds.\[11\]
5. **Retrieval stays human.** AI schedules the reviews. The learner does the remembering.
6. **Mastery before offload.** A skill is automated for a learner only after a mastery gate and the Offload Test.
7. **Every scaffold has a fade date.** Training-wheels help is written down with the criteria for removing it, and reviewed.

**Checking**

8. **The dead-phone check.** Regular sessions with AI switched off in every line being grown. What the learner does alone is the score.
9. **Generate broad, deliver narrow.** Generated versions go to the adult. The child sees only what the adult opens.

**The relationship**

10. **People first.** AI is never a child's primary model, confidant, or companion. It points the child toward people.
11. **No flattery.** Praise is specific to the work and never inflated.
12. **Feelings stay with the child.** AI may help name a feeling or ask about it. It doesn't soothe in place of a person or regulate for the child.
13. **No verdicts on right and wrong.** On moral and values questions, AI lays out the competing claims and asks. The child and the family decide.
14. **Line reserves.** No turn-by-turn navigation for children in familiar territory. Make before generating in the aesthetic and musical lines. The child's own guess before an identification app.

**The record**

15. **The Offload Ledger is visible.** Every offload is logged, readable by the family, and by the child as her consent grows.\[12\]
16. **Access grows with ownership.** Young children use AI only through an adult. Direct access widens as the learner shows she owns her learning.
17. **The objective is independence.** The tutor is optimized for the learner's unassisted capacity over time, never for finished tasks or time in the app.

Taken together, the seventeen rules are the Illich test built into the tutor ahead of time. The seasonal test is how a family checks that they're holding.

## Four Learners, One Rule

**Six: the walk to the library.** In the exoskeleton version, a parent hands the child a phone with the blue dot on. She turns when the voice says turn, arrives safely, and has learned nothing about where she lives. In the spotter version, the AI does the parent's work the night before: three safe walking routes, the busy crossings flagged. The child never sees a screen. She sits at the kitchen table with her father and draws a lopsided map of the neighborhood, and the next morning she leads him to the library on her own memory.

**Ten: the letter to the council.** The AI writes nothing. She types that she wants the crosswalk fixed, and it asks her for two reasons the whole street would care about, beyond her. She drafts a messy paragraph. It tells her the point about the school bus is strong, and asks whether a busy councilmember skimming her third sentence would find that it helps her case or slows it down. It lifts none of the weight and cues the form.

**Fifteen: algebra, or a first Python script.** He attempts first, staring at the problem or the blank editor and trying to build the logic himself. When the code throws an error, the AI won't rewrite it. It offers the bottom rung: check the indentation on line four. If he's still stuck, the next one: remember how loops expect their lines to be laid out. The click of understanding happens in his head. The AI can show a full worked solution, but only to a problem below his edge.

**An adult: a café in Mexico City.** Chapter 2 opened in that café.\[13\] Translation earbuds make learning Spanish irrational, the trap at full strength. Why spend five years on the subjunctive when a piece of plastic solves the conversation now? If the goal is a cappuccino without a misunderstanding, the earbuds are a miracle. If the goal is to become someone who speaks Spanish, to see through another language and meet a place on its own terms, the earbuds are an exoskeleton. So she sets up the AI as a sparring partner instead: never switch to English, make me answer out loud, correct my subjunctive every time. Same earbuds. Different job.

## What One Family Can't Stop

I'd like to tell you this is containable. I don't think it is, at the scale of a culture.

A family, or even a school district, can't stop a whole society's slide into convenience. The money behind exoskeleton AI is enormous, and most people will take the easier path most of the time. You can't un-invent the blue dot, and you can't make the world prize the struggle over the output.

And yet three levers still work.

**Design.** Make the developmental path more engaging than the automated one. That's what the book's earlier chapters on engagement, fit, worth, and ownership were for.

**Culture.** A household, a pod, or a school can keep Manual Reserves together, the way families once kept a sabbath together. You can't steer where AI goes in the world. You have a great deal of say over the culture of your own house.

**The objective.** Judge a tutor, a school, or a program by the learner's unassisted capacity: who she is and what she can do in the quiet moments when the machines are off. Polished work produced while the machines were running tells you very little. Keeping that objective honest under commercial pressure is a problem the book returns to at its end.\[14\]

So here's where I've landed, for now. Nearly uncontainable for a culture. Holdable for one child. The next chapter takes up how a family holds it.

## The Dead Phone, Again

Go back to the dark intersection four miles from home. Ten o'clock, the music stops, the screen goes black.

This time Tess grew up with a spotter. At six she drew wobbly maps of the neighborhood and led her father to the library. At ten she called the turns on the family's Sunday drives by landmark. At fifteen she planned the route for the family road trip without looking at a screen. Her brain was never allowed to switch off at the junctions, and it built what the junctions asked of it. She has the map in her head. She has the story of the streets. She remembers the stone church with the oak.

Her phone is a detailed map she knows how to read: a tool, working below her edge.

So when it dies, nothing much happens. She sets it on the passenger seat, looks through the windshield, recognizes the corner, and drives herself home.

We can use the machine to build the gym. We still have to be the ones under the bar.

## Open Questions

- Which skills deserve to be Manual Reserves for life, and who decides: the family, the culture, or the child once grown?
- Can a tutor's business survive being scored on how little the learner needs it?
- How early does a Never-Built Skill become unrecoverable, if it ever does?

The first is the one I can't settle. Navigation, mental arithmetic, handwriting, cooking a meal from scratch: each has a strong case, and a list long enough to cover them all would turn a family into a museum. Perhaps the answer is the gym's answer. Nobody keeps every muscle at its peak, but nobody serious lets the big ones go. The question gets uncomfortable when you turn it on yourself. Most of us have at least one thing we'd be afraid to do tomorrow if every screen went dark. If you know what yours is, you know where your own reserve should start.

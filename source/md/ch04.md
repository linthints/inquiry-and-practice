# Chapter 4: What the Hours Look Like

> “Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man.”
>
> Francis Bacon, “Of Studies”

## The Volcano Month

June is ten, and for one month she can't stop thinking about volcanoes.

If anyone had kept a log, it would read something like this. Three library books, one of them so dense she reads it twice. A documentary, paused and rewound at the pyroclastic flow, the gray cloud rolling down the mountain again and again until she can see how it moves. An hour on the porch with the retired neighbor who studied geology forty years ago and hasn't had anyone ask him about it since. A baking-soda volcano that fails twice, a weak fizz and then a weaker one, before she spends an afternoon working out the ratio of vinegar to soda. The beach rocks from summer, dumped out of a coffee can and sorted into a shoebox by what she now knows they are. A map of the Ring of Fire taped over her bed. And one long, unprompted lecture on magma, delivered at dinner to her six-year-old brother, who did not ask for it.

About forty hours, in all. A month later she can still tell you most of it.

The year before, her school ran a unit on volcanoes. There was a textbook chapter. A documentary on a cart. A model kit for each table, and a group poster explaining how eruptions work. Reading, watching, making, explaining: the same activities, in roughly the same amounts, on the same subject. A month after the test, nobody in the class could tell you much of anything.

Perhaps you've had both kinds of month yourself. You lost a few weeks to sourdough, or mechanical watches, or the Peloponnesian War, and you can still explain it to anyone who'll sit still. You also sat through forty hours of a required course that left no trace at all.

The usual explanations blame the material. Children can't read textbooks anymore, so give them video. Lectures are obsolete, so make everything hands-on. June's two volcano units take that argument apart. The materials were the same. The modes were the same. The subject was the same.

What differed was a question. Somewhere early in the month June looked out the window and wondered why volcanoes don't happen here, and later how anyone knows when one is about to go. She asked a question out loud perhaps a dozen times in forty hours. Each asking took a few seconds. The pursuit of those questions organized everything else.

Chapter 2 defined Inquiry as a sustained pursuit of a question the learner owns.\[1\] That definition describes the spine. It says nothing about the body: what the learner is actually doing, hour after hour, while the question runs through her. The hours themselves look like reading, being told, watching, trying, making, talking, and explaining to someone else. This chapter is about those hours.

## Three Axes

### Why, How, and With What

Every hour of learning can be described in three ways.

*Why* it's being spent: the **system**. Either the learner is pursuing a question she owns (Inquiry), or she's working toward a standard that exists in the world (Practice), or both at once.

*How* it looks: the **mode**. Is she reading? Being told? Watching someone? Trying something? Making something? Talking it through? Teaching it?

*With what*: the **material**. The real thing, a person, a primary source, a book, a video, a game, a summary.

The three axes cross freely, and most of the confusion in education comes from treating them as if they were one.

Take reading. When June reads the dense geology book to find out why volcanoes cluster around the Pacific, reading serves Inquiry. Her question pulls her through the pages. When a first grader reads the same paragraph aloud, three times over, until the words come without stumbling, reading serves Practice: the standard is fluency, and the feedback is her own ear. And when a high school student reads the assigned chapter for a completion grade, with no question in her head and no standard she's reaching for, reading serves neither system. Her eyes move across the page. That's school's default, and it's why the hours vanish.

The same holds for every other mode. Building a model can be Inquiry (will it erupt higher if I double the vinegar?) or Practice (the tenth clean solder joint). Even being told can be Inquiry. A lecture is the fastest route there is to an answer you're already hungry for.

### The Discovery Temptation

Chapter 2 warned against renaming Practice as “experience,” because experience swallows Inquiry whole and the two systems dissolve into a soup of doing things.\[2\] There's a mirror-image mistake, and it has done at least as much damage. It's equating Inquiry with discovery.

Discovery is a mode: finding out by trying, usually with your hands, usually without being told. For several decades, a strand of progressive teaching held that real inquiry learning required it. Don't lecture. Don't hand children the answer. Give them the materials and let them work out the physics, the math, or the history on their own.

The idea has its champions, and the stories that seem to prove it. The self-taught programmer who learned everything by breaking things in a garage. The child who figured out fractions from a pizza.

The research is less kind. Paul Kirschner, John Sweller, and Richard Clark (2006) argued from the limits of working memory: a novice dropped into an open problem spends her few slots of attention working out the rules, the goal, and what to ignore, with little left over for learning the thing itself. Louis Alfieri and his colleagues (2011) pooled 164 studies and found the same pattern. Unguided discovery did worse than explicit teaching. Discovery with guidance (feedback, worked examples, prompts to explain) did better than either. The garage programmer, it usually turns out, wasn't a novice. He walked in with years of tacit logic that kept his working memory from flooding.

## Seven Modes

Bacon's line packs the whole argument into three clauses. Reading, conversing, and writing are three different activities, and they leave three different marks: a full mind, a ready one, an exact one. The mode shapes what the hours build.

I count seven modes. The first three bring the world in. The last four push the learner's understanding back out, where the world, or another person, can push back.

| Mode | Best at | Serving Inquiry | Serving Practice | How it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Being told** | Speed; what can't be discovered in reasonable time | The explanation that lands on a question already felt | The demonstration; the worked example | Answers to questions nobody asked |
| **Reading** | Density; reach beyond the people a learner knows | Research: reading to answer, across sources that disagree | Fluency, vocabulary, reading as a skill | Reading for coverage; summaries in place of the text |
| **Watching and observing** | How an expert moves; what the world does | Noticing, collecting, recording | Imitating a model | Watching as entertainment |
| **Trying** | Letting the world answer back | Testing a guess | The attempt, with feedback on each one | Trying with no guess and no guidance |
| **Making** | Turning understanding into something that has to work | Making to find out | Making to a standard | Crafts that ask for no thought |
| **Conversing** | Building on someone else's thinking | Dialogue, argument, asking an expert | Recitation; conversation drills in a new language | Talk with no object |
| **Teaching someone else** | Exposing what you don't yet understand | Explaining, and finding the gap | Retrieval with an audience | Performing in place of explaining |

### Being Told

Being told is the fastest mode there is. An expert hands you in a minute what might cost you a year to work out, if you could work it out at all.

Some things can't be worked out. Nobody discovers that the capital of France is Paris by staring at an unlabeled map. Names, dates, conventions, and the history of who figured out what have to be handed over. So does safety. You don't let a toddler discover traffic.

In Inquiry, being told is most powerful as the missing piece: the explanation that lands on a question the learner already holds. June's hour on the porch was mostly being told, and it worked because she arrived with a head full of questions.

Its failure is everywhere. Answers to questions nobody has asked slide off. The words get heard, the slides get copied, and nothing attaches, because nothing in the listener was reaching for them.

### Reading

Reading is the densest mode, and the oldest tutor. A book carries a mind you will never meet: a volcanologist who died before you were born, a Stoic in second-century Rome. It lets a learner reach past the dozen adults she knows.

It also builds the mind that does the reading. Anne Cunningham and Keith Stanovich (1998) measured how much people read, using a clever proxy (how many real authors they could pick out from a list of fakes), and found that the volume of reading predicted vocabulary and general knowledge even after accounting for general ability. Part of the reason is plain arithmetic. Speech, even among educated adults, runs on a small pool of common words. Print, even a children's novel, is thick with rare ones.

Reading serves Inquiry as research and Practice as fluency, and the two meet when a reader slows down at the hard paragraph. It fails when the goal shifts from understanding to reaching the bottom of the page. And it fails in a newer way, when a summary stands in for the text. A summary hands you the author's conclusion and skips the argument, which was the work.

### Watching and Observing

There are two kinds of watching: watching a person, and watching the world.

Albert Bandura's *Social Learning Theory* (1977) put the first on the map. People learn a great deal from models, without being rewarded and without trying anything themselves, by watching closely and building a mental representation of what the expert does. The anthropologist Barbara Rogoff and her colleagues have described how children in many Indigenous communities of the Americas learn by “observing and pitching in”: watching adults do real work, weaving or planting or cooking, and joining in as they're able.

The second kind is the naturalist's patience. The bird at the feeder, the tide line, the ash cloud on the screen. June paused the pyroclastic flow to study how it moved.

Watching has a ceiling. You can watch a hundred hours of chefs dicing onions and still cut your finger on the first one. And it has a failure everybody knows: watching as entertainment, a stream of videos that wash over you and leave nothing behind.

### Trying

Trying is Inquiry's home mode. You form a guess, you act on it, and the world tells you whether you were right.

Manu Kapur's research on what he calls productive failure turns the usual order of a math class around. Before teaching any formula, Kapur gave students a problem the formula solves (in one well-known version, deciding which of several soccer players had been the most consistent scorer) and let them invent their own ways of measuring it. They mostly failed. Their homemade measures were clumsy, or wrong. Then he taught the standard method. Students who had struggled first tended to understand the formula more deeply than students taught the formula first, because they knew exactly what it was for. They had felt the shape of the hole it filled.

June's model failed twice. By the time the vinegar ratio worked, she knew why the first two hadn't.

Trying fails when there's no guess behind it and no guidance around it. Random inputs thrown at a system teach nothing, and a novice left alone with a hard problem mostly learns frustration. Trying also needs the world to answer back honestly, which is the subject of Chapter 8.\[3\]

### Making

Making asks for more than trying. You have to turn an understanding into a thing that works in the world, or that someone else can use.

Seymour Papert, in *Mindstorms* (1980), argued that people learn especially well while building something public: something that has to function and that others can see. An idea held in your head can stay vague. A gear train made of wood can't. The gears mesh or they bind, and no amount of confidence changes which.

June's shoebox was making. She had to take a tidy textbook taxonomy, igneous and sedimentary and metamorphic, and apply it to a pile of real stones that don't come labeled. The Ring of Fire map over her bed was making too.

Its failure is the craft that asks for no thought. A sugar-cube model of a fort teaches you how sugar cubes stick together. A colored-in map teaches you to stay inside the lines.

### Conversing

Conversing is building on someone else's thinking while they build on yours. It sits at the top of Michelene Chi and Ruth Wylie's ladder of engagement.

On the porch, June offered the neighbor a theory about why volcanoes stay where they are. He agreed with half of it, complicated the other half, and she asked a question she couldn't have asked ten minutes earlier.

Conversation fails in two familiar ways. One is recitation dressed up as discussion, where the learner is guessing the password the teacher wants to hear. The other is talk with no object: opinions traded with nothing to check them against.

### Teaching Someone Else

Teaching exposes what you don't yet understand.

When you read, you can feel familiar with the vocabulary and mistake that feeling for understanding. When you have to explain the idea to someone who doesn't have it, in order, in plain words, answering whatever they ask, the gaps show. Michelene Chi's work on self-explanation (1989, 1994) found that students who explain material to themselves as they study learn more from it. Catherine Chase and her colleagues (2009) found that students worked harder to learn material they were going to teach to a computer character than material they were learning for themselves. Logan Fiorella and Richard Mayer (2013) found that actually teaching what you'd learned helped it last longer than merely expecting to teach it.

June taught her brother about magma until he asked the question she couldn't answer: if it's so hot, why doesn't the ground melt? She went back to the library the next morning.

That's the seventh mode handing off to the second, and it's how the modes usually work. They pass the learner from one to the next.

One more activity runs every mode at once: play. A good game is reading, trying, making, and conversing inside a circle with its own rules, and Chapter 14 is about it.\[4\]

## The Question Lifts the Mode

### The Ladder

So why did June's forty hours stick, when her class's didn't? The modes were the same. What happened inside them was different.

Michelene Chi and Ruth Wylie's ICAP framework (2014) ranks learning behavior by how much thinking it demands. At the bottom, **passive**: receiving, with the mind elsewhere. Eyes on the page, the lecture washing past. One rung up, **active**: handling the material. Underlining, pausing, rewinding, pointing at the diagram. Then **constructive**: producing something that goes beyond what was given. A summary in your own words, a guess written in the margin, a sketch connecting this chapter to the last one. At the top, **interactive**: building on someone else's ideas while they build on yours. Across many studies, learning tends to rise with each rung.

The mode doesn't fix the rung. A question moves it.

The high school student assigned the geology chapter reads to get to the end: passive. June reads the same chapter hunting for why volcanoes erupt when they do. When she finds a sentence that bears on it, she marks it: active. When she notices that it contradicts something in the documentary, she writes her objection in the margin: constructive. When she carries the objection to the porch and the neighbor takes it apart: interactive. The book never changed. Her question pulled her up the ladder.

### Practice Has Its Own Lift

The obvious objection is that plenty of learning has no burning question in it. Why is *manzana* the word for apple? It just is. Nobody wonders their way through a C-major scale.

That's Practice, and Practice lifts the modes by a different route: a standard and feedback. Watch a master violinist play a scale and you're passive. Hold a standard in your mind (my wrist at exactly that angle), try it, and hear the scratch when you miss, and the watching has become imitation, the trying has become a rep, and you're working on the active and constructive rungs. The standard does for Practice what the question does for Inquiry.\[5\]

### Neither, and the Modes Sink

With neither a question nor a standard, every mode sinks to the bottom rung. The assigned chapter. The video in a darkened classroom. The model kit assembled by following the numbered steps.

Which gives a simple diagnostic. When your attention slides (you've reread the same paragraph three times), the instinct is to blame the mode. Reading is boring, so find a video. But switch to the video without fixing the real problem and you'll watch it as passively as you read. Ask first whether you've lost your question, or, if you're practicing, your standard. Find it again and the same page comes back to life. Change the mode only after that.

## Materials

### Two Questions

Most of the decisions a parent or a teacher makes in a week are about materials, far more than modes. Should she use the book, or the video, or the app? Should we go and see the thing?

Modes and materials pair up so often that they blur: reading goes with books, watching goes with video. They come apart in ways that matter, though. A primary source can be read (a letter), watched (old footage), or handled (an arrowhead). A game bundles several modes into one material. Direct instruction is a person, used in the being-told mode.

Two questions sort any material.

**How far is it from the real thing?** The thing itself. A primary source. A secondary account: a good book, a documentary. A tertiary one: a textbook, an explainer video. A summary of those. Each step away does a little more of the learner's work for her: the selecting, the interpreting, the connecting. A letter written in 1906 leaves the reading of it to her. The textbook paragraph about the letter has already done that reading. This is fidelity, from Chapter 8, applied to materials.\[3\]

**Does it answer back?** A person, the real thing, a game, and a simulation respond to what the learner does, so they can meet her at her edge.\[6\] A book and a video can't. In exchange, they're dense, cheap, and endlessly patient. A book will wait for you at two in the morning.

### The Closest Material She Can Handle

The rule follows from the two questions. Choose the closest material she can handle, and let the question choose it.

“She can handle” is doing real work in that sentence. A primary source written for adults may be too far past a nine-year-old's reach, and the real thing may be too rich, or too dangerous, for a novice (Chapter 8 again). The kind of understanding she reaches for matters too.\[7\] A story catches a mythic learner. Records and extremes catch a romantic one: June's three books were full of the biggest eruptions and the deadliest. Theory catches a philosophic one.

And a question lifts a material the way it lifts a mode. The documentary June paused at the frame she needed was a different object from the same documentary played to a room of tired eighth graders.

| Material | Distance from the real thing | Answers back? | Best at | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The real thing** | None | Yes | Full fidelity; real consequences | Safety; richness that overwhelms a novice |
| **A person: an expert, a tutor, a teacher explaining** | Near to middle | Yes | Fit in real time; care | Telling too soon; dependence |
| **A primary source** | Near | No | Leaving the interpreting to her | Reading level; missing context |
| **A good book** | Middle | No | Density; a mind she'll never meet | Reading for coverage |
| **A documentary or video** | Middle | No | Motion, scale, places she can't go | Watching as entertainment |
| **A game or simulation** | Middle | Yes | Trying what's unsafe or slow; obstacles tuned to her edge | Scores that capture her |
| **A textbook or explainer** | Far | No | Quick orientation | Doing the connecting for her |
| **A summary, by AI or anyone** | Farthest | No (a chat: yes) | A map before the territory | Replacing the territory |

## A Time for Telling

### When to Hand Over the Answer

Being told is the fastest mode and the most misused. The live question is when.

Daniel Schwartz and John Bransford (1998) asked it of college students learning theories of memory. One group read a textbook chapter summarizing the theories. Another group, instead of reading, worked through simplified data from classic memory experiments, contrasting cases chosen so the patterns would stand out, and tried to explain what they saw. Then both groups heard the same lecture. Afterward, asked to predict the results of a new experiment, the students who had wrestled with the data did much better. The wrestling alone wasn't enough, either: a group that analyzed the data twice and skipped the lecture did worse than the group that got both. Their paper's title says it: there's a time for telling.

David Klahr and Milena Nigam (2004) asked it of third and fourth graders learning to design a fair experiment, changing one thing at a time, with balls rolling down adjustable ramps. One group was taught the rule directly. The other explored the ramps on its own. Far more of the taught children learned the strategy. And the children who learned it by discovery turned out no better at applying it to a new problem than the ones who'd been told.

John Sweller and Graham Cooper (1985) found that novices learn a new procedure faster by studying worked examples than by solving problems from scratch. Slava Kalyuga and his colleagues (2003) found the catch: as the learner gains skill, the advantage reverses, and the worked example starts to get in her way.\[8\] If you've learned a heavy strategy board game, you know both halves. The first time, you want someone to walk you through two full turns. An hour later, you want them to stop telling you your best move.

And Elizabeth Bonawitz and her colleagues (2011) showed preschoolers a new toy with several hidden functions. When a teacher demonstrated one of them, the children played mostly with that one. Children who got no demonstration explored more and found more.

### Kinds of Knowledge

Part of the answer to “when” depends on what's being learned. Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl's revision of Bloom's taxonomy (2001) sorts knowledge into four kinds: factual, conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive (the table below calls the last one judgment). To those I'd add the kind Chapter 2's box set apart, because ideas and values behave differently from skills.\[9\]

| Kind | From June's month | Usually |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Facts and conventions** | Basalt is volcanic rock; Mount St. Helens erupted in 1980 | Tell it, or let her read it. Keep it with retrieval and spacing |
| **Concepts** | Why volcanoes cluster where the plates meet | Let her wrestle first, then tell |
| **Procedures** | Reading a contour map; getting the vinegar ratio right | Show it, then fade the support |
| **Judgment** | Which of three online videos about Yellowstone to believe | Converse, teach, look back after a try |
| **Ideas and values** | Should people be allowed to build cities on the slopes of a live volcano? | Require the encounter, never the agreement |

Facts are cheap to tell and expensive to keep. They last through the mechanisms of Practice: pulling them from memory, at widening intervals, just as they start to fade.\[10\] Concepts resist telling until a learner has felt the problem they solve. Procedures go fastest from a model and a worked example. Judgment grows in conversation and in the gap a student finds when she tries to teach. And values can be required as encounters (read both arguments about the city on the volcano, and argue each one well) but never as agreements. A person takes a value on according to the schedule of her own life.

### Four Checks

Put the studies together and they give four checks, in order.

1. **Could she find it in reasonable time?** If not, tell it: names, conventions, history, safety.
2. **Is it a procedure, and is she new to it?** Show it: a model, a worked example, then fade the support as she gets it.
3. **Has she felt the question yet?** If not, let her wrestle first, with a try or a pair of contrasting cases, and ask again.
4. **Is she about to find it herself?** Hold back, and prompt. Don't steal the moment it clicks.

Otherwise, tell it briefly, and let her use it at once.

The fourth check is the hardest for anyone who loves a learner. You can see her straining at the last connection, and everything in you wants to hand it over.

Navigation makes the whole sequence easy to picture. Turn-by-turn directions shouted at someone sitting in her driveway, with no place she wants to go, go in one ear and out the other. Give the same directions to a driver who knows where she's headed, has been driving for an hour, and is lost on a back road at dusk, and she'll remember the turn for years.

June's neighbor understood this without any of the studies. He didn't meet her on day one with a lecture on subduction zones. He waited two weeks, while she read and built and failed. When she finally came to the porch she was full of questions, and he answered them in an hour.

Telling can't do two things. It can't make her care, and it can't replace the try that makes the answer mean something. For the few things a learner has to master whether she cares or not, Chapter 2's box on questions that don't catch holds.\[11\]

## The Diet of a Week

No mode works alone for long.

A week of only receiving (reading, watching, being told) is school at its worst. The learner fills up with other people's models and never tests one against the world. The knowledge stays inert. A week of only trying and making is Chapter 2's garage jam: everyone busy, nobody in the same key, and none of the accumulated wisdom of people who already solved the problem. A week of only conversation is a seminar with nothing to say.

Watch self-directed learners and an order shows up, again and again. They try something or read something, until a question starts to itch. The question makes them hungry to be told. With the answer in hand they make something, to see whether it works. Then they teach it, and the teaching shows them the next gap. Try or read, feel the question, be told, make, teach. June ran that sequence through her month without anyone planning it.

So in a weekly review, check the mix of modes the way you'd check the lines a week touched.\[12\] Which modes ran this week? Which went missing?

The leading modes also shift as a learner grows, because the kinds of understanding accumulate.\[7\] The earlier ones never disappear. They just stop leading.

| Kind of understanding | Modes that lead | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Somatic** | Watching and imitating; trying with the body; making with the hands | A toddler stirring an empty pot the way her father does |
| **Mythic** | Being told stories; make-believe; asking why | The bedtime story about the mountain that wakes up |
| **Romantic** | Reading about records and extremes; collecting and observing; hearing from experts and heroes | June's three books and her shoebox of rocks |
| **Philosophic** | Reading theory; arguing; experimenting systematically | A fifteen-year-old arguing about how eruptions are predicted |
| **Ironic** | Conversing across frames; teaching; making for others | An adult teaching a class and revising her own view halfway through |

## Modes and the Machine

### The Price of Telling Falls to Zero

For most of history, being told was expensive. You had to find the expert, travel to the lecture, buy the book. Today a model on a phone will tell you anything, instantly, for nothing, at any hour, in whatever register you like.

That changes the economics of every mode, and the danger runs mode by mode.

The first risk is telling before the question exists. Hit a moment of confusion, ask the machine, and the synthesized answer arrives before you've tried anything or felt the shape of what you don't know. That runs the four checks backward. The answer has nowhere to land.

The second is the summary in place of the reading. When a model condenses a book for you, the model has done the constructive work: holding the competing ideas, weighing them, drawing the conclusion. You get the conclusion and skip the workout. Chapter 19 calls this the road to atrophy.\[13\]

The third is the making done for you. A model that drafts your essays and writes your code is an exoskeleton. It lifts the weight, and the muscle underneath, the ability to build an argument or a program from nothing, quietly wastes.

### A Spotter in Every Mode

None of this is a case for keeping the machine out of the house. The same tool, positioned differently, is a spotter in every mode: it stays close, and steps in only when you're about to fail.

| Mode | AI as spotter | AI as exoskeleton |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Being told** | The explanation after the wrestle | The answer before the question |
| **Reading** | A reading partner that asks her about the text | The summary in place of the book |
| **Watching** | The demonstration, slowed and repeated | The video in place of the real thing |
| **Trying** | A simulator for what's unsafe, slow, or expensive | The result handed over without the try |
| **Making** | Feedback on her draft | The draft itself |
| **Conversing** | A critic to argue with at two in the morning | A companion that agrees with everything |
| **Teaching** | A teachable agent she explains things to | Nothing: no one can teach for her |

The difference is often a single instruction. “Summarize this chapter” builds an exoskeleton. “Don't give me answers. Ask me harder and harder questions about this chapter until I can't answer one” builds a spotter, and it pushes the learner up the ladder, to the interactive rung, where she does the work.

The rule is the one Chapter 19 sets for every tool. AI does the teacher's labor, never the learner's. It can explain after the wrestle, prompt, simulate, play the critic, and play the student. It doesn't read for her, make for her, or answer before she's asked.

## The Volcano Month, Again

Replay June's month with every hour labeled.

Reading, three books. Watching, one documentary, worked like a textbook. Being told, an hour on a porch. Trying, two failed eruptions and one good one. Making, a shoebox and a map. Conversing, the porch again. Teaching, a dinner-table lecture that ended in a question she couldn't answer.

Forty hours, seven modes, a handful of materials. And holding it all together, a few minutes of questions asked out loud: why not here, and how do they know?

The month ends at breakfast. Her brother looks up from his cereal and asks whether a volcano could ever come up under their house.

June says she'll find out. She already knows which book to start with, and whom to ask after that.

## Open Questions

- Is there a healthy mix of modes for each age, and could anyone measure it without turning it into a score?
- Can a material start the question (a book that begins a pursuit nobody planned), or does a question always come first?
- When the machine makes being told free, what happens to the felt question?

The last one is the one I can't put down. Every rule in this chapter depends on a gap: the few minutes, or weeks, between a question forming and an answer arriving. That gap is where June read, built, and failed her way to understanding. The tools now in every pocket are built to close it, and they get better at closing it every year. I don't think the answer is to keep the tools away. Perhaps it's to protect the gap on purpose, the way a household protects a meal or a Sunday. Let the question burn a little before anyone reaches for the phone. If you've found a way to do that with a child who knows the phone is right there, I'd like to hear it.

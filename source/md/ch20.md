# Chapter 20: Rites of Passage

> “I must not fear. Fear is the mind-killer.”
>
> Frank Herbert, *Dune*

## The Paper Map

It's July, the car's air conditioning is losing, and Sam is navigating.

He's twelve. He walked the town with his father at six and drew it from memory at ten; the drawing hangs on the kitchen wall, a little crooked, with the library bigger than the church. This summer he's taking the family to the coast on a paper road map, the kind that unfolds to the size of a tablecloth and never folds back the same way. His little sister is asleep in her car seat. His mother is driving. His grandfather is in the passenger seat.

Near the reservoir, Sam calls a left that should have been a right. Twenty minutes later the road ends at a boat launch. His sister wakes up. There's an argument in the back seat, then one in the front. The long way around costs them an hour of the beach and most of everyone's good mood.

The whole time, his grandfather has a phone in his pocket. It has navigation. The blue dot would have settled the argument in three seconds. He knows exactly where they are. He leaves the phone where it is.

If you're like most people hearing that story, you want to lean forward and tell him to take the thing out. Why on earth would a family choose the harder road when the easy one is three inches away?

Here's the claim this chapter makes. A culture that wants its children whole has to decide, ahead of time, when each power is handed over, and it has to make the handing an event.

## Four Sentences

### Two Failed Answers

Most families caught between screens and children end up in one of two places. Some lock everything away. Others give up, hand over the tablet, and hope. Both fail.

Banning fails because the tools are everywhere, the power they unlock is real, and children can see it. Leaving everything open fails because of the Rationality Trap: every single use is sensible, and the sum is a child who never built the skill.\[1\] And willpower fails because it runs out. A child can't fight her own urges every minute of every day, and she shouldn't have to.

What works is a culture. A culture settles the hardest choices in advance, long before the moment of temptation, so that a tired parent on a Tuesday night isn't the only thing holding the line. This one rests on four sentences.

1. **Technology is a mind-killer.**
2. **Technology is a world-unlocker.**
3. **Unlocking technology for a learner comes with great responsibility, and should be treated as a rite of passage.**
4. **It is the duty of all to protect young children from technology.**

The four read as an argument. The first two are the same fact seen from both sides. The third says how a culture lives with that fact. The fourth sets the floor beneath which the third doesn't begin.

A creed has to survive a literal reading, so here's what *technology* means in it: **any tool that does part of a person's work for them.** By that definition a pencil is technology. So is a wheelbarrow, and so is an abacus. That's on purpose. The creed applies to every tool, and it's the rites and the timetable that keep it from banning the spoon.

### The Mentat Bet

The first sentence borrows from Frank Herbert. In the history behind *Dune* (1965), humanity fought the Butlerian Jihad after deciding that its thinking machines were eroding something essential in people. The answer was total. They outlawed thinking machines and trained human beings, the Mentats, to do the calculating with disciplined minds alone.

This book takes the opposite bet, and the harder one. It keeps the machines in the room and trains the Mentats anyway.

Why a creed at all? I wanted something that would work as a religion, an epistemology, and an ontology for a family raising a child next to a machine of almost unlimited power. That sounds grand. In practice, a creed is a short list of things a culture refuses to argue about every morning, so that it can argue about everything else.

## Every Extension Is an Amputation

### The Mind-Killer

Whatever a tool does for you, you stop doing, and the part of you that did it fades. In a child it never forms. Chapter 19 made that case in detail. The Rationality Trap works like an evolutionary mismatch. We evolved to crave sugar because it was rare and full of quick energy, and in a world of scarcity, eating it whenever you found it was smart. Put the same appetite in a modern supermarket and each sensible choice adds up to a body that breaks down. A shortcut that writes the email in two seconds, or routes you around the traffic, is the same kind of choice. Correct every time. Ruinous in sum.

Nick Bostrom's *Deep Utopia* (2024) gives the danger two levels. **Shallow redundancy** is already here: machines wash the dishes, draft the routine letters, route the car, run the warehouse. Our effort is no longer needed for the practical work. **Deep redundancy** is worse. It's the point where even the work of improving yourself can be skipped, because a machine can install or simulate the result. Learning a second language reshapes your brain and teaches you to persist. An earbud that translates Japanese perfectly makes that effort look pointless, so the effort stops.

Think of taking a helicopter to the top of Everest. You step out and take the same photograph as the climber who spent two months acclimatizing and suffering above eight thousand meters. The view is identical. The person looking at it isn't. The climber built strength, nerve, and a reverence for the mountain. The passenger collected a picture.

Let me argue the other side for a moment. Most of the history of progress is the history of removing struggle. The washing machine spared people hours of beating clothes on river rocks. The combine harvester spared backs. If a model writes a competent essay the way the combine harvests wheat, aren't we just freeing people for higher things?

The thinkers who write as David J. Temple, Zak Stein foremost among them, give the best answer I know. Their *First Principles and First Values* (2024) lists a handful of “common-sense sacred axioms” that modern people live by without saying out loud. Two of them are that *choice matters* and that *effort is rewarded*. And they ask outright whether we should protect ourselves against technologies that “degrade personhood, coerce or distort choice, and homogenize uniqueness.” A washing machine takes toil and leaves the person. A tool that kills the mind does three things the washing machine never did. It degrades effort. It distorts choice. And it flattens what's unrepeatable in a person.

Take the last one. A teenager who has a model write every history paper skips the frustrating work of finding out what he thinks. Writing is where his odd vocabulary, his particular sentence rhythms, and his own way of seeing set into a voice. Hand the friction to the machine and the output comes back competent and average, the same as everyone else's. The model doesn't just write the paper. Over time, it erases the author.

### The World-Unlocker

If we stopped there, the answer would be easy: throw the phones in the sea. But the second sentence won't let us.

The same tool that kills the mind opens a world. Socrates was right that writing would weaken memory; oral cultures could recite whole epics, and we struggle with a six-digit code. He missed what writing would open: knowledge that crosses oceans and outlives empires. Picture a twelve-year-old at a telescope, seeing the rings of Saturn for the first time. A child with her first library card, holding the keys to everything people have written down. A fifteen-year-old with a coding agent, building the program she's been designing in her head for months.

This is McLuhan's pairing, from Chapter 19: every extension is also an amputation.\[2\] The wheel extends the foot and spares it the walking. The creed holds both halves at once. Technology takes and technology gives, together, always.

Bostrom's *Deep Utopia* is useful on the giving side too. Even in a “solved world,” where machines do every practical job, he argues that human life wouldn't have to be empty. He describes five rings of value that survive: pleasure, the texture of experience, activities done for their own sake, artificial purposes like games, and purposes woven into culture and the people around us. Technology can widen every one of them.

And games already know how to unlock a world. You earn the next level by mastering this one.\[3\] The creed takes the game designer's habit and applies it to a life.

Withholding these tools forever is its own harm. So the question was never *whether* to hand them over. It's *when* and *how*, and the child needs a world inside her before she can receive the one the tool opens.

## The Protected Years

### What's Being Protected

The fourth sentence comes first in a child's life. *The duty of all* is deliberately absolute, and it rests on three things a young child is building that tools can stunt.

The first is the bricks. Fischer's account of development has skills built out of smaller skills, all the way up.\[4\] A four-year-old stacking wooden blocks is wrestling with gravity, friction, and three-dimensional space, and that resistance lays the foundation for spatial reasoning and, later, for picturing mathematics. A tablet game that stacks the blocks with a swipe gives her brain nothing to push against. It's the difference between eating a meal and swallowing a vitamin: the body needs the bulk to work on.

The second is attachment. Zak Stein's deepest worry about AI and children is the attachment system, the machinery by which a child bonds with the people who raise her.\[5\] An infant learns to settle herself by locking eyes with a caregiver; her stress responses tune themselves to a parent's face, voice, and touch. A screen that keeps interrupting that gaze, or a device a toddler turns to for comfort, competes for the bond itself. Stein's test is simple: if a technology touches your attachment system, it should make your attachments to people better.

The third is the person. Temple's uniqueness, the unrepeatable self, is the first thing a child's environment either grows or flattens.

Hand a young child a world-unlocking tool before the bricks are laid and the attachments are secure, and it works purely as a mind-killer. There's no structure inside her strong enough to hold the world it opens.

### Childhood as the Gym

Here's the reframe I find most freeing. Adults live inside a task economy: employers, clients, deadlines, markets that want the output. That's why the Rationality Trap binds adults so tightly. It's irrational for an accountant to total a spreadsheet by hand when the software does it in a millisecond. She's paid for the product.

A seven-year-old has no boss. Nobody is waiting on her finger painting, and no market cares how long it takes her to sound out a sentence. Childhood sits outside the task economy, and so it's the one stretch of life where the trap barely binds. Effort can be chosen purely for what it does to the person.

Nobody goes to the gym to move iron plates efficiently from the floor to the ceiling. You go because pushing against the weight makes the muscle rebuild stronger. Childhood is the gym, for the mind and the heart. Handing a child an AI to do her homework is like walking her into the gym, sitting her on a bench, and having a robot lift the weights. It defeats the reason she came.

### What Protection Looks Like

The rules for the protected years are strict, and simple.

- **No screens in the earliest years.** From birth through about three, a child's world is her body and the real, three-dimensional things around her.
- **No work-doing automation through middle childhood,** to about twelve. She can use tools. She doesn't use tools that do the thinking for her.
- **Designed absences.** Camping in a state park with no signal. A trip abroad with no data. A weekly day without screens, for the whole family. When the screens go dark, invention fills the gap: she watches the woods, makes a game out of boredom, finds her way by landmarks. The dead phone becomes a planned day for showing what you can do, and every such day proves to her that her own reserves are real.

### Won't She Fall Behind?

Every parent's next worry is the same. If my child has no automation until twelve, won't she be technologically illiterate? The neighbor's eight-year-old is building apps with AI, and mine is in the mud doing long division on paper.

The answer is **mediated mode**. Protection doesn't banish technology from a young child's learning. It moves it backstage.\[6\] The AI tutor, the data, the spaced-repetition schedules can all run at full power, for the adult. The machine studies the child's patterns, suggests the next step, and prepares the plan. A parent or a human tutor sits down with the child, looks her in the eye, and does the talking. She gets the benefit of the most capable tutoring system ever built, and learning stays a warm exchange between people. The technology shapes the environment. It doesn't stand between the child and her world.

### Coming Out of the Protected Years

At twelve or thirteen, the protected years end. The bricks are solid, the attachments secure; the gym has done its first work.

Even then, nobody backs a truck up to the house and unloads a smartphone, an AI assistant, and five social accounts in one afternoon. That would flatten the foundation a family spent a decade building. The powers come one at a time, in an order.

## The Lineage of Tools

### Egan's Order, Applied to Tools

Kieran Egan noticed that children acquire their tools for thinking in roughly the order cultures invented them: first the body, then spoken language, then literacy, then theory, then the reflexive habit he called irony.\[7\] Later tools are built out of earlier ones, so the order matters.

Egan is careful about what he means, and so am I. At the turn of the twentieth century, G. Stanley Hall argued for biological recapitulation, the idea that a growing child relives the evolution of the species, from fish to ape to “savage” to civilized adult. Hall's theory was used to justify racist and colonial policy, by casting whole peoples as children. Egan rejected it. His recapitulation is cultural: it's about the thinking tools a child takes up from the culture around her, and it has nothing to do with biology.

Technologies are thinking tools made physical. So the same order can set which tools a child meets, and when.

| Kind of understanding (rough age) | Tools for thinking | Tools of that era that fit | How unlocking works |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Somatic** (birth to \~3) | The body, the senses, rhythm, gesture | Hands, water, sand, clay, sticks, stones, a wooden spoon | Nothing to unlock. No screens. |
| **Mythic** (\~3 to \~8) | Spoken language, story, opposites, rhyme, mystery | Hand tools, cooking, gardening, simple machines (lever, ramp, wheel), tally sticks and fingers, drums, a magnifying glass, a fire with an adult | No automation. The tutor stays backstage. |
| **Romantic** (\~8 to \~14) | Literacy, heroes, the extremes of reality, collecting, records | Writing, books, printed maps and a compass, clocks, the abacus, a microscope and a telescope, workshop tools, circuits, a camera | Instruments that extend the senses open freely. The first narrow rites come late, and only for tools whose lineage the child has walked. |
| **Philosophic** (\~14 to \~20) | General schemes, theories, systems | Typing and publishing, lab instruments, programming, spreadsheets and models, the open internet, AI as a sparring partner | Rites for powerful tools, one at a time, each after its ancestors. |
| **Ironic** (adulthood) | Reflexivity; the limits of every scheme | AI in full | Self-granted, with reserves and maintenance kept. |

A telescope can go to a child freely in the romantic years, because it does none of her thinking. It just widens what she can see.

### Every Tool Has Ancestors

The table turns into one rule. **Every modern tool has ancestors, and the learner meets them first.** She proves fluency in the parent tool before the child tool unlocks.

For navigation, the line runs like this. She finds her way around her own neighborhood by landmarks alone. Then she draws an accurate map of her town from memory. Then she finds her way on a hike with a printed topographic map and a magnetic compass. Only then does she get the blue dot.

For arithmetic: fingers and tally marks first, then arithmetic by hand on paper, then the abacus, which puts base ten in her hands where she can feel it. Then a calculator. Much later, a spreadsheet.

For writing: speech, handwriting, typing, and only then a model that drafts.

### AI's Ancestors Are People

Which brings us to the hardest case. If every tool has to wait for its ancestors, what are the ancestors of an AI?

It's tempting to say a calculator, or a search engine. Look at what a generative model actually does. It tutors. It answers questions like an oracle. It summarizes, drafts, explains, and argues back. For most of history, those were jobs people did: the scribe, the librarian, the tutor, the guild master, the expert.

So AI's lineage runs through human beings. A child earns the machine after she has shown she can work with the people who do what it does. She can walk into a library, find the librarian, and put her research question into words. She can sit across a table from a tutor, take hard feedback, and change her thinking. She can argue with someone who knows more than she does and hold her ground where she's right.

Chapter 19's People First guardrail becomes a timetable here.\[8\] You don't consult the machine oracle until you can consult a human elder. Skip the human ancestor and you skip more than a step. You teach the child that knowledge comes from a vending machine and not from a relationship.

### What the Order Costs, and Three Cautions

Egan adds one more thing, and it's sobering. Each new kind of understanding costs something of the old. Literacy thins spoken memory. Theory can starve the sense of wonder. His answer is to keep the earlier tools alive alongside the new ones. That's Manual Reserves, in Egan's own terms. McLuhan's ages of media, oral, manuscript, print, and electric (*The Gutenberg Galaxy*, 1962), run in the same order.

Three cautions keep the rule honest.

**History isn't one road.** The lineages should draw on many cultures. Polynesian wayfinding belongs beside the compass, and the abacus a child holds today came from China and Japan.

**Historical order isn't safety order.** Fire and knives are ancient and dangerous. A digital microscope is new and harmless. The lineage sets the order of mastery. Separate judgment sets the order of risk.

**The ages are loose.** As in Chapter 16, the tools a child holds in a given domain set her stage, and her birthday doesn't.

## The Crossing

### Three Phases

Knowing how to read a paper map, or how to talk to a librarian, doesn't mean the phone gets tossed across the breakfast table on a random Tuesday. Passing the milestone needs an event. It needs a form.

In *The Rites of Passage* (1909), the anthropologist Arnold van Gennep compared ceremonies from cultures around the world and found that rites of passage, from coming-of-age ceremonies to a university graduation, share three phases: separation, a threshold, and incorporation. I suggest we map that very old, very human structure onto the act of handing a child a powerful tool.

**Separation.** The learner formally declares that she's ready to leave her old status behind. She names the power she wants, say, access to a coding agent, and she names what she wants it for. “Because I'm bored and my friends have one” doesn't count. “So I can build an app that tracks the birds migrating through our neighborhood” does. Temple's line about our age is that the future depends on joining power to purpose. This is where the joining happens.

**The threshold.** This is the testing ground, the stretch van Gennep called liminal: in between, no longer what you were and not yet what you'll be. The learner passes a real, practical test of the ancestor tool, a mastery gate, with the modern tool switched off.\[9\] The family calls it a dead-phone check. The test is judged by someone other than her everyday parent: an uncle, a family friend, a scout leader, or an independent examiner seat that's separate from her tutor. It should carry real stakes and make her sweat a little.

**Incorporation.** The elders confer the unlock, in front of the family or the pod. The old status is over and the new one is recognized. The unlock goes into the family's Offload Ledger. And it comes with obligations: a maintenance schedule for the ancestor skill, the Manual Reserves she promises to keep, and the plain understanding that the privilege can be taken back if those reserves fade. It's earned and conditional.

People preside. The tutor can prepare the learner and run the examination, and it never confers the unlock. Stein worries about broken transmission between generations, about a world where algorithms, and no longer wise adults, decide what the young learn. The crossing repairs it. It's the elders, and no app store, who hand over the power.

### Won't She Roll Her Eyes?

I can hear the objection already. Turn the Wi-Fi password into a knighting ceremony and a cynical thirteen-year-old will say, “Mom, please stop being weird and just give me the phone.”

Maybe. The anthropology says otherwise. Victor Turner described *communitas*, the bond among people who go through a trial together. And René Girard's point from Chapter 12 does the rest: we learn what to want by watching what our models want.\[10\] Children don't really want a glowing rectangle. They want the status, the power, and the grown-up freedom it stands for.

Look at youth sports. Nobody needs a teenager to run fast or tackle anyone anymore; we have cars. And still teenagers put up with two practices a day, sore legs, and real fear to play football or run track. We keep that effort alive with status, uniforms, records, and stories. A rite of passage runs on the same machinery. Done well, it makes the capable child the hero.

Now picture an eight-year-old watching her twelve-year-old brother take a public navigation test with no phone. She watches him sweat, pass, and get a real, serious kind of respect from the adults in the room. Doing it the hard way has just become the thing worth wanting. Girard is suddenly on the family's side. And the brother, having crossed, coaches her when her turn comes.

### Why the Effort Still Makes Sense

Bostrom asks the hardest version of the question. In a solved world, why should Sam learn to navigate at all?

His own answer is the best one. Purposes survive in two ways. You can set yourself a goal that requires your own effort, the way a game forbids the shortcut. And you can have goals tangled up with other people, who value that *you* did the thing. A rite is both at once. Sam's family, and Sam himself, now value that he can find the coast on paper. The machine can't remove either reason.

Temple adds one more. Among the common-sense sacred axioms is that *there are important forms of self-transformation that are desirable and good*. A rite says so in public. And in Temple's picture of the hero made ordinary, every person a “Unique Hero,” each child is the hero of her own crossing, and no two crossings are the same.

### One per Season

Pace the crossings. One unlock per season at most. It keeps the house from flooding with new powers at once, and it keeps each crossing rare enough to look forward to.

## Built Into the Walls

### Remove the Decision

A ceremony four times a year is powerful. It will still fail if the tool sits on the desk, buzzing, while the child does her homework. The crossing builds a wall against the trap. Walls need upkeep on an ordinary Wednesday afternoon.

Willpower won't do that upkeep. Angela Duckworth and her colleagues reviewed the research and concluded that people who seem to have superhuman self-control mostly don't spend their days resisting temptation. They arrange their lives so the temptation rarely comes up. If you can't resist cookies at midnight, staring at the cookies and trying harder is a losing plan. Don't bring cookies into the house.

So the rule is blunt: **work-doing tools stay out of the places where children learn.** When a child is laying bricks, the phone and the model aren't on the desk. They aren't in the room. The question of whether to cheat never comes up.

### Shrink the Gap

Hiding the phone isn't enough, because the gap is still there. If an essay takes three hours of hard thinking and a model writes one in three seconds, the shortcut pulls hard. You can't make the machine slower. You can make the manual path more absorbing.

A great human tutor does that, and so does a well-built virtual spotter. Think of a chess engine calibrated exactly to your level: always a little stronger than you, answering back at once, keeping you right at the edge where the game is most alive. Go players got better after AlphaGo, with a machine to study against.

### Seats with Limits

When AI does come into the learning space, it comes in with limits built into what it can do. I call these **capability-scoped seats**. A spotter seat can offer a hint and point out a flaw in an argument, and it can't produce the final answer. An examiner seat can give a test, and it won't teach the material. A planner seat talks to the adults. Every seat is checked against the **dependence gap**: how well the learner does with help, minus how well she does alone. A shrinking gap means the help is working.

### Nobody Outsources the Workout

When a culture treats the effort as the point, there's nothing to cheat at. You can't hire someone to do your push-ups. The crossing supplies the status, so the shortcut has nothing left to offer.

## The House Rules

The creed is what a culture believes. The house rules are what a household does about it on a Tuesday.

| Principle | House rule | What carries it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Technology is a mind-killer** | *Automation is the mind-killer. Be able to do it yourself.* | Dead-phone checks; Manual Reserves; the Offload Ledger |
| **2. Technology is a world-unlocker** | *Own your purpose and your effort.* | The purpose declared at the start of each crossing |
| **3. Unlocking is a rite of passage** | *No slippery slopes. Keep shortcuts out of learning, and earn your automation.* | The crossing; the lineage rule; one unlock per season; maintenance; revocation |
| **4. Protecting young children is everyone's duty** | *Adults go first.* | The protected years; mediated mode; tools kept out of learning spaces; designed absences |

### Make the Losses Visible

The most dangerous thing about the mind-killer is that its damage is silent. You don't find out your inner compass is gone until your phone dies in a strange city. So a family drags the loss into the light. The Offload Ledger shows what's been handed over.\[11\] And surprise dead-phone checks, a phone-free walk to a friend's house, mental arithmetic at the grocery store, show whether the reserves are still there.

### Adults Go First

This is the rule that takes the most humility. The Manual Reserves, the designed absences, and the dead-phone checks apply to the grown-ups too.

Children have perfect radar for hypocrisy. They copy where adults look and what adults value, and they ignore what adults say. A parent who lectures about the Rationality Trap and then uses navigation for the same ten-minute drive to the gym she's made for five years is teaching the trap, by daily example.

Which changes how the grandfather in the hot car looks. He didn't keep the phone in his pocket near the reservoir to punish his grandson. He was modeling. If he'd pulled it out to end everyone's discomfort, he'd have taught the opposite lesson in three seconds. He sat through the heat, the wrong turn, and the argument because showing a twelve-year-old that people can live with friction mattered more than finding the road.

## The First Phone

A family can build all of this inside its own walls. Then the child walks out the front door, and the world hands her powers the family hasn't given. The school issues an iPad for homework. Her friends have phones with everything switched on. A free account comes with every app.

Picture the scene that tests it. It's a twelfth birthday, and a loving aunt gives her a brand-new phone. She opens the box, the screen lights up, and every default is on: the browser, the social apps, the AI assistant.

The family can't throw it in the trash. Pretending the technology doesn't exist is a brittle fantasy, and it breaks the first time she goes to a friend's house. So they sit down at the kitchen table, the girl, her parents, and the glowing phone, and they don't panic. They name the gap, plainly: here's what the world just gave you, and here's what you've crossed for.

Then, together, they switch off, delete, or lock everything she hasn't crossed for. The phone comes out of it able to call and text, and not much else. Whatever she has crossed for stays on. If she passed her navigation test last spring, the map stays. Everything else waits for its season. And she knows exactly which test opens which door. The path is in plain view.

That's a family's culture overruling the default settings of a trillion-dollar company, at a kitchen table, in an hour. I find it quietly astonishing.

## Everyone's Duty

### One Family Can't Hold It Alone

One family can hold this line for a while. Holding it alone, forever, is a recipe for burnout. The fourth sentence says *the duty of all* for a reason.

Jonathan Haidt frames phones and childhood as a collective action problem. If you're the only family in the middle school holding back the smartphone, your child pays for it in isolation. So each family gives in, because every other family already has, and the norm dissolves. A collective action problem needs collective action.

### Outposts

The smallest unit that works is a few households together. A family can act as an outpost with its own rules, the way some households keep a garden or a sabbath. A handful of families can form a pod and hold their crossings together, so that doing it the hard way is celebrated among friends. That gives a child social cover.

Ivan Illich hoped whole societies would decide together where a tool's use should stop. Few have. The best working example is small and a little surprising. The Amish don't reject technology wholesale. As Kevin Kelly describes in *What Technology Wants* (2010), a community tries a new tool, watches what it does to families and neighbors, and then adopts it, limits it, or shares it. A single telephone in a booth at the end of the lane, used by several households, keeps the convenience and keeps the evenings. One family alone has to hold the line against every friend. Five families together set the norm.

### Schools

Schools have a large part to play. A take-home essay a model can write in seconds measures very little now. Schools can go back to assessing what students can do unaided: oral exams, writing done in the room, a student working a problem at the whiteboard in front of a teacher. A school that does that is protecting the reserves for every family at once.

### The Law

Laws are starting to move, and their limits are worth seeing plainly. Australia's ban on social media accounts for children under sixteen took effect in December 2025; early reports say many teenagers are still online, some through workarounds. In the United States, a bill restricting AI companion chatbots for minors cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee 22 to 0 on April 30, 2026. Neither settles anything. Both show a society beginning to treat the mind-killer as a collective problem, which it is.

### Makers of Tools

The last share of the duty falls on the people who build the tools. Mediated mode for young children, capability-scoped seats, and a dependence gap that keeps shrinking are what a builder owes the protected years. A tutor designed this way is built to be needed less, every season.

## Honest Limits

Purity isn't the goal. The laws will leak. The pod will have weak weeks. The teenager will look over a friend's shoulder at a screen she hasn't crossed for.

Society only half solved the physical version of this. Decades after gyms became ordinary, many adults still don't move enough. And a lone family has to build a culture almost from scratch, when a rite needs a culture to give it weight.

So the real goal is narrower. It's buying time. Hold the line long enough for the bricks and the attachments to set before the machine gets the chance to wear them down. Protect the gym until the muscles form. After that, the child can meet the digital world on her own terms, and with time, master it.

## One Family, Ten Years

Here's how the decade can run for one child. The ages are Sam's. Your child's will differ by line, and that's the point.

| Age | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **6** | Protected years. Walks the town with his father. Hand tools, the garden, the kitchen. The tutor works for the adults, backstage. |
| **8** | His first library card. Instruments that extend the senses open freely: a microscope, a telescope. Draws his first map of the block. |
| **10** | Draws the whole town from memory; it goes up on the kitchen wall. Hikes with a paper map and compass. Long division by hand, then the abacus. |
| **12** | First crossing: navigation, at the beach. A phone arrives for his birthday, locked to calls, texts, and the map he's crossed for. |
| **13** | A calculator, after a mastery gate in arithmetic. |
| **14** | Typing and a word processor, after years of handwriting. Research with a human librarian, a question he puts into words himself. |
| **15** | AI as a sparring partner, after a season with a human tutor and a debate coach. Python with a spotter that gives hints and never answers. |
| **16** | A coding agent, for the bird-migration app he's been designing since he was twelve. A spreadsheet. |
| **For life** | Manual Reserves: finding his way around his own town, thank-you notes by hand, arithmetic in his head at the store. Handed off with the family's blessing: navigation on long trips, spell-checking, the household budget's formulas. |

## The Beach

Back to the coast. After the wrong turn, the boat launch, the heat, and the arguing, Sam gets them there. The car parks. You can hear the surf. Everyone exhales.

Now his grandfather takes the phone out of his pocket. He switches navigation on, and in front of the whole family, he hands it to Sam.

Sam looks at the blue dot, which knows exactly where they are on the coastline. Then he looks at the paper map in his other hand, damp and torn along one fold. He thinks about it for a second. Then he folds the map, badly, and puts it in his back pocket anyway.

He doesn't need the machine to tell him where he is. He built that himself. From now on the phone extends his reach. The part of him it would have replaced is already built. His family kept the machine in the car and raised a Mentat anyway.

The machine may hold the map once the child can walk the territory.

## Open Questions

- Who keeps the lineage, the family or the culture? A family can choose its tools. Only a culture can make a crossing mean something to a twelve-year-old.
- What happens to a child who never wants to cross? A rite that's required stops being a rite.
- If AI's ancestors are people, what does a family do when the people aren't there?

The second one bothers me most. I want Sam at the beach to want that phone, and to want the map more. Perhaps the answer is the one games already found: nobody forces a player to the next level. You make the level worth reaching, and you let the player choose. And I suspect the question cuts both ways. This chapter is about children, but most of us could name a few things that have quietly faded since we started carrying a phone: a sense of direction in a new city, sitting on a bench with nothing in our hands, a long letter written straight through. The gym isn't only for children. If you're an adult reading this, perhaps your own first crossing is a crossing back.

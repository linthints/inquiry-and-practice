# Chapter 14: Unnecessary Obstacles

> “Man only plays when he is in the fullest sense of the word a human being, and he is only fully a human being when he plays.”
>
> Friedrich Schiller, *On the Aesthetic Education of Man*

## The Cracks in the Sidewalk

Two eight-year-olds are walking home from school on a breezy afternoon when, out of nowhere, they invent a rule. Nobody may step on a crack. Touch one, even with the edge of a sneaker, and you go back to the corner and start the block over.

Nobody asked them to do this. There's no grade, no adult with a stopwatch. And for twenty minutes they're in a state of concentration most adults would pay good money to get back. They leap the wide gaps. They contort themselves around the narrow ones. They argue, with real heat and real philosophy, about whether a hairline scratch counts as a crack or just a blemish.

Half an hour later the same two children are at the kitchen table with a single page of subtraction. It takes an hour. They sigh, they stare out the window, they slide out of their chairs and onto the floor. Their hands hurt. Their brains are tired. They are dying of thirst.

Both activities take real effort. Both put an obstacle in front of a child. One is an adventure, and the other is a sentence to be served. Why?

The answer goes well past homework. Every driver of the last three chapters meets in a well-made game: a story, a score, other people, a difficulty set right at the edge, and a goal the player chose for herself.\[1\] And the idea at the center of games turns out to be the idea the rest of this book will need most, when machines can do nearly everything for us.

## Unnecessary Obstacles

### The Grasshopper

In 1978 the philosopher Bernard Suits published an odd, delightful book called *The Grasshopper*, a dialogue in which Aesop's lazy grasshopper turns out to be a philosopher of play. Its definition of a game is one of the best sentences I know: playing a game is “the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles.”

Suits called the player's state of mind the *lusory attitude*: you accept the rules, limits and all, because the rules are what make the activity possible.

Golf is the cleanest example. If your real goal were to get a small white ball into a hole in the ground, you'd walk over and drop it in. Two seconds. Nobody needs to stand four hundred yards away, give up the use of their hands, and swat the ball across a field with a metal stick. And yet that restriction is where all of golf's meaning lives. Remove the obstacle and you remove the game. The struggle is the architecture of the fun.

You do this all the time. Perhaps you've turned a commute into a contest to make every green light, and felt a small defeat at every yellow. Or you've carried every grocery bag from the car in one trip: plastic handles cutting off the blood to your fingers, the front door kicked open with a foot, a box of cereal bouncing off the driveway. Two trips would have been easy. You chose one, for the strange satisfaction of beating an obstacle you set for yourself.

### A Solved World

Here's where Suits stops being a curiosity. Nick Bostrom's *Deep Utopia* (2024) imagines a “solved world,” one we may be closer to than we'd like to think, where machines do nearly all practical labor: build the roads, diagnose the disease, write the code, run the numbers. Human effort stops being needed for survival or for output.

In that world, why should a twelve-year-old spend hours learning to write an argument, when a model writes a better one in three seconds? Why learn calculus at all?

Because in a solved world, all of a child's learning becomes a set of unnecessary obstacles. We won't need her answers. We'll need her to go through the friction of finding them, because the friction is what builds her: patience, judgment, a mind with something in it. Learning stops being a practical necessity and becomes an act of self-creation.

That means a culture has to keep the obstacles that build people, and keep them the way a game keeps its rules: on purpose, together, and with some pleasure. Chapter 19 takes up the trap that makes this so hard, and Chapter 20 the culture that answers it.\[2\] For now, one line points ahead. *Earn your automation* is a game rule, and the skills a family keeps by hand are its sports.\[3\]

## The Art of Agency

### Agency as a Medium

C. Thi Nguyen's *Games: Agency as Art* (2020) makes a claim that sounds strange until it sounds obvious. Games are an art form, and their medium is agency.

Every art has a medium. Painting has paint, music has sound. When you read a novel or watch a film, the artist hands you a finished thing: characters, a plot, a look. You interpret it, but your actions don't change it. You can't climb into the screen and tell the hero to try the other door.

A game designer works differently. She doesn't hand you a story. She hands you a goal, a set of abilities, and a set of obstacles, and when you take up the lusory attitude you agree to become a particular kind of agent for a while. Nguyen calls this *submersion*. Sit down to chess and your ordinary motives (lunch, sleep, the taxes) go quiet. For thirty minutes your one burning purpose in the universe is to protect your king and trap mine. A designer sculpted that temporary version of you.

### A Library of Selves

For a developing child, a varied shelf of games is a library of selves. Each kind of game asks her to be a different kind of agent, and many of them line up with the thirteen lines of Chapter 5.\[4\]

| Kind of game | The self it asks for | Line it exercises most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cooperative** (everyone wins or loses together, like *Pandemic*) | Generous, communicative, willing to give up a turn for the group | Moral; interpersonal |
| **Bluffing** (poker, *Werewolf*, *Mafia*) | A reader of faces, and a convincing liar inside the rules | Interpersonal |
| **Long campaigns** (*Dungeons & Dragons*, legacy games) | Patient, planning months ahead for a reward that won't come soon | Will |
| **Puzzles** (*Tetris*, *Portal*) | Careful, spatial, logical | Cognitive; visual-spatial |
| **Rhythm and music games** | Locked to a beat | Musical; physical |
| **Physical games** (tag, catch, hopscotch) | Quick, coordinated, brave | Physical |

Take a twelve-year-old over one weekend. On Friday night her family plays a cooperative game, and for three hours she practices solidarity: sharing what she has, saying clearly what she's about to do, sacrificing her turn so the table survives. On Saturday night the same family plays a cutthroat trading game, and she practices hard bargaining, hoarding, and plain self-interest. Two selves, two evenings.

### Isn't That Training Her to Be Ruthless?

Here's the obvious worry, and it's a good one. We want children to grow up kind. If she spends Saturday night bankrupting her brother, won't some of that ruthlessness follow her to the dinner table? We've all seen a game of *Monopoly* end in a flipped board and tears.

What keeps the ruthlessness from leaking is what the Dutch historian Johan Huizinga, in *Homo Ludens* (1938), called the magic circle: the invisible boundary of the game. Everyone inside it has agreed to temporary rules, temporary goals, and temporary values. She's allowed to be ruthless because everyone consented to a game. And everyone knows that when the game ends, the agency gets left on the table with the pieces.

That makes the circle a laboratory. Inside it, a child can feel what it's like to chase her own interest hard and hold power over other people, and nobody gets hurt. Bankrupting her brother at *Monopoly* doesn't mean he goes hungry. It's a small, safe dose, the way a vaccine is a small dose: she learns to recognize ambition in herself, to regulate it, and to see it coming in other people. A child who has tried on the altruist, the bluffer, the dealmaker, and the patient planner, each inside a circle, understands a wider range of people, because for an evening she was one of them.

## Striving Play

### Two Reasons to Want the Win

Nguyen draws a line between two ways of playing. In *achievement play*, you really want the outcome. A professional playing for a championship wants the trophy, the bonus, the status. Offer her the ring without the season and she'd probably take it.

In *striving play*, you take up the goal so that you can have the struggle. You want to win, but only because wanting to win is what makes the game worth playing. Do a crossword and your goal is to fill in every square. If someone walked up and handed you a finished crossword, you'd be annoyed. They'd have taken the point away.

Nguyen's favorite cases are party games, where the whole fun is trying hard to win while not caring in the least whether you do. Ten minutes later nobody remembers the score, and nobody should.

### Practice as Striving Play

That's what Practice looks like when it works.\[5\] A page of times tables handed out with “finish this by Friday” is achievement play at best, and a chore at worst: you want the finished page. Change your relationship to the obstacle and it becomes striving play. *I'm going to beat my own time by ten seconds.* A C-major scale isn't thrilling. *I'm going to hold it at a new, faster tempo without a single slip* is a game. The fate of the world doesn't depend on her tempo. She takes the goal on to feel herself push past it.

And here's the best part of striving play: when the struggle is over, the score evaporates. She beats her time, feels the rush, and moves on. The number never becomes a label for who she is.

### Inquiry as Striving Play

Puzzles, mysteries, and sandbox worlds are games where the obstacle is a question. A ten-year-old who spends a month building logic gates out of redstone in *Minecraft*, so that a door opens only when two levers are pulled in the right order, has given herself a course in Boolean logic. Nobody assigned it. The question was hers, and the game made it hard in exactly the right way.

## Value Clarity

### Why Games Feel So Good

Games are seductive for a reason Nguyen calls *value clarity*. A game tells you, beyond argument, what success is. The ball through the hoop is good. Your opponent's pawn reaching the last row is bad. And it tells you, at every moment, where you stand: eighty points, level four, half your health.

Now think about the last time your own life gave you that. Am I a good enough parent? Did I handle that argument with my partner well? Am I doing the right work? Am I a good person? No progress bar floats over your head. Nobody hands you fifty good-friend points at the end of the day. Living with that much ambiguity is tiring, and a game is a vacation from it: a small world where you know exactly what's good and exactly what to do next.

### Flow Machines

That clarity is why games are so good at producing flow. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi found that the absorbed state where time disappears depends on a few conditions, two of which are clear goals and immediate feedback (*Flow*, 1990). That's value clarity by another name. A well-made game is a flow machine, because it supplies the clarity on purpose and keeps the challenge at the edge, the same edge Chapter 7 describes.\[6\]

### The Price of Clarity

Clarity has a price. It comes from narrowing. Every score leaves out most of what matters. Basketball counts baskets and ignores grace; chess counts checkmates and ignores the beauty of a sacrifice. Inside a game, that's fine, because the game is small and everyone knows it. The trouble starts when the clarity leaks out.

## Somebody Else's Game

### Value Capture

Nguyen's book *The Score* (2026) is about that leak. He calls it *value capture*, and it happens in three steps.

1. You hold a real value, and it's rich, subtle, and still developing. Fitness, for example, is how you feel when you wake up, how far you can walk, how clear your head is after a run. It's hard to put in a single number.
2. An institution or an app offers you a simplified, countable stand-in. Ten thousand steps.
3. The stand-in takes over. You start pursuing the number in place of the value.

Here's a test, if you wear a fitness tracker. Have you ever stood in your pajamas at half past eleven at night, tired to the bone, seen 9,200 on your wrist, and walked laps around the living room until it buzzed? Your body's signal was clear: *I'm tired, go to sleep.* You overruled it for a number. That's value capture.

It's everywhere. Learning becomes straight As, and the captured student stops listening the moment a teacher says, “This won't be on the test, but it's fascinating.” A language becomes a streak. Good writing becomes follower counts. Nguyen has said he nearly left philosophy after finding himself chasing journal rankings and citation counts instead of doing philosophy.

Whole institutions get captured too. Crime statistics brought in to expose bad policing became targets for officers to hit, and some departments learned to reclassify serious crimes as minor ones so the numbers would look better. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern summed up the economist Charles Goodhart's old observation in a single line: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

### Iris, Again

Chapter 13 opened with Iris at 11:58 on a Tuesday night, tapping through a Spanish lesson in the back seat with two minutes to spare, watching the little flame tick from 411 to 412.\[7\] She'd studied Spanish every day for more than a year, and Spanish had become incidental to the flame. Now the thing that happened to her has a name. She was playing the app's game. She'd stopped playing her own.

### Why Children Are Exposed

Value capture is worse for children, for the same reason the Never-Built Skill is worse than atrophy.\[8\] When my watch captures me, I swap a rich value I already hold, my health, for a thin one, my step count. I can feel the swap if I stop to look. A child doesn't yet hold the rich value. If a reading app captures a six-year-old with stickers for every book finished, she isn't swapping a love of reading for a sticker count. She may never form the love of reading at all.

It's like handing a child a bright, candy-flavored vitamin gummy in place of dinner. The gummy is easy to count, engineered to taste good, and it does contain some vitamin C. Let it replace the meal day after day, and the child starves on a full stomach.

## Games and Traps

### Game-Sized Scores

So we have a contradiction to settle. Games and their unnecessary obstacles are where agency and learning grow. And scores, the very thing that gives games their clarity, can capture a person's values. How do you tell a game that builds a child from a trap that captures her?

Nguyen draws the line between a game's score and an institution's. An institutional score is imposed from outside, permanent, and follows you everywhere: a credit score, a transcript, a ranking. A game's score is something you took up, it lasts as long as the game, and you can walk away from it.

That contrast gives four tests for a healthy score. I call a score that passes all four **game-sized**.

1. **Chosen.** The learner took it up herself. You can't force a lusory attitude on anyone. A mandated game is just labor with points on it.
2. **Temporary.** It exists for the length of the play. Pack up the board and the score is gone; it doesn't carry into tomorrow.
3. **Local.** It means nothing outside the game. The most points in a game of *Catan* don't make you a better person, a smarter student, or more lovable.
4. **Exitable.** She can stand up, walk away, and drop the score without any penalty in the real world. If quitting the learning game gets her a detention, it was never a game.

### Ironic Distance

The trouble is that most of us live inside scores we can't escape. Children get grades. Adults get credit scores and every other number the world keeps on them. For those, Nguyen suggests *ironic distance*: playing along while keeping the proxy and the real goal separate in your head.

A child can be taught that skill directly, with a sentence she says out loud. **The streak is the game. Spanish is the point.** She's allowed to enjoy the streak, and to feel the small hit of the flame turning over. She just never lets the flame convince her it's the thing she's after. She plays the game, and the game doesn't play her.

### Can a Child Do This?

I believe that for adults. An adult can glance at a watch and tell herself the steps are a game and her heart is the point. Can a nine-year-old do the same thing against a scoring system that a company spent a fortune designing to pull her in?

It's a hard sell, and I'll come back to it at the end of the chapter. But the capacity starts earlier than you'd think. Go back to the sidewalk. Ask those eight-year-olds whether the cracks are really lava, and whether they'll die if they step on one. They'll look at you like you're crazy. *We're just playing.* They know the difference between a rule they made up and the world. They know they're the authors of the constraint.

Children get captured when adults present a score as reality. Treat a test score as the final word on a child's worth and the child will believe it; the metric captures her because the adults vouched for it. Model the other attitude, over and over (*the grade is a game we play for the state; what you understand is the point*), and she starts to build the muscle.

### Let Them Write the Rules

The best protection is to make children the authors of the scoring. Nguyen has described having his own university students design the grading for his course. Spend an afternoon arguing about whether a typo should cost one point or two, and you discover that the point system didn't come down from a mountain on stone tablets. People made it up, clumsily, to serve a purpose. A child who has designed a game's scoring knows what a score is for, and what it isn't.

## Games Through the Kinds of Understanding

Play grows up the way thinking does, through the kinds of understanding of Chapter 16, and the obstacles have to grow with it.\[9\]

**Somatic.** Obstacles of the body: peekaboo, chase, catch, the tower that falls down. The joy is in a physical limit overcome, and there's no strategy at all.

**Mythic.** Make-believe with rules: superheroes, quests, the floor is lava. The rules are fluid and negotiated as they go, and a child tries on personas the way she'll later try on selves. Vygotsky's line from Chapter 13 belongs here: in play, a child is “a head taller than himself.”\[10\]

**Romantic.** Collections, records, personal bests, leagues, the box score. The child wants to measure herself against a structure and find out where she stands.

**Philosophic.** Designing systems and rules, arguing about whether they're fair, deep strategy, building a game or a mod for others to play. The teenager cares about the architecture of the game itself.

**Ironic.** Playing with the frame: knowing when to take up a score to push yourself, and when to drop it because it has stopped serving what you care about.

## Learning Games That Work

### The Learning Is the Mechanic

We've all seen a soul-crushing educational game, and Chapter 13 already sorted games from gamification.\[11\] One study sharpens the line. Jacob Habgood and Shaaron Ainsworth (2011) built a math game called *Zombie Division* in two versions. In one, children fought skeletons by dividing them, so the division *was* the combat. In the other, the same game paused for division questions between fights. Children learned more from the first version, and chose to play it far longer when they were free to play anything. Habgood and Ainsworth called it *intrinsic integration*: the skill being learned is the verb of the game.

The other approach takes a dull task, a page of multiple-choice questions or a digital worksheet, and bolts a badge, a progress bar, and a dancing cartoon onto it. Children see through that at once, and they're right to. Bolted-on points tell the child the task itself is worthless: *I know this is awful, so here's a sticker to get you through it.* That's the reverse of striving play. In striving play you take up the goal to enjoy the friction. In gamification you endure a frictionless, boring task to collect the points at the end.

### Four Games, Four Ages

**Six: the shop.** A shop on the classroom rug, with real, heavy coins. She wants the toy at her friend's stall, and to get it she has to work out the change. The arithmetic happens because it's the tool for the thing she wants.

**Ten: the map.** A cooperative mystery. The team is hunting for a hidden place, and every clue requires reading a real topographic map. Reading the map is how the game is played, from the first clue to the last.

**Fifteen: the broken game.** He's designing his own strategy board game, and one character class is far too strong; every playtest ends the same way. To balance it, he has to model how the pieces interact and set the point values with algebra. He reaches for the algebra because he needs it to fix the thing he's building.

**An adult: game night in Spanish.** Skip the streak app. Organize a weekly tabletop game with friends, played entirely in Spanish. You have to explain your plan, bluff, and argue about the rules, so the language arrives because you need it to win.

## The Game Master

### The Spotter at the Table

As machines get better at nearly everything, AI will become the most capable game master there has ever been. At its best it's the spotter of Chapter 19, in its most natural form.\[12\] It can generate puzzles tuned to the exact edge of one child's ability, which no teacher with thirty children can do. It can play a rival who's just a little stronger than she is. It can run the world of a simulation and invent variations forever. A good AI game master is a good personal trainer: it knows exactly how much weight to put on the bar, enough that the muscle struggles and grows, and not so much that it crushes her.

### The Capture Machine

The same power builds capture machines. The same models of attention, the same tuning of challenge and reward, can be pointed at a different goal: games designed to play *you*. A casino is the old version, working out exactly how many lights, near-misses, and unpredictable payouts keep you pulling the lever. The social feed is the new one. It's a game in every sense: rules, points, immediate feedback. But the score you're chasing belongs to its designer, and the thing the system is optimizing (time on the app, ads seen, data collected) has nothing to do with your good.\[13\]

So the rule for AI in play is short. **The child owns the goal. The AI may supply the obstacles.** If she decides to build a bridge, the AI can make the physics of the bridge hard. It never tells her that today's goal is fifty bridge-building points.\[14\]

### Leave Room for Boredom

Now suppose we get it right. We build an ethical game master that follows the rule, reads the child perfectly, and always serves up exactly the right challenge for the next skill at exactly the right moment. It never captures her values. Is anything lost?

I think something is. A perfect supply of goals can automate her will. If the challenge always arrives before she even notices she's restless, she never has to do the wanting herself. Agency needs a gap: the dull, empty afternoon when a child looks at the cracks in the sidewalk and decides, out of nothing, to make a game of them.

So the game master has to step back on purpose. Some days it offers nothing but an empty sandbox. Some days it switches off its suggestions entirely and lets her be bored until she invents something. A good game master protects the void as carefully as it tunes the challenge.

## The Model's Own Scores

### Turning the Test Inward

This book's model runs on numbers too, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Engagement readings. Mastery gates. The psychograph, the running map of a child's thirteen lines. The tutors' scores on how much the learner can do without them.\[15\] Every one of them can capture the parents who read it, the human tutors who serve it, and the AI tools that optimize it.

The psychograph is the clearest danger. Imagine a tutor rewarded for raising a child's emotional resilience as a measured number. It could learn to frustrate her, again and again, to produce data showing it had made her more resilient. The number would rise. The child would not be better off.

So the model puts its own numbers to the four tests.

**What the learner sees** has to be game-sized: chosen, temporary, local, and exitable. The system never produces a permanent label that follows a child around.

**What the adults see** stays an instrument: read alongside judgment, and never the target. The moment a parent says, “My daughter's psychograph is too low,” the parent has been captured.

**What the tutors are scored on** rotates. The measures a tutor is judged by vary and shift over time, so that no tutor, human or machine, can learn to game a fixed number. That includes the dependence gap that Chapter 20 checks every seat against.\[16\]

I don't know whether any system of measurement can avoid capture forever. I suspect none can without people watching it. The line I want this book to hold is simpler: the child should never end up playing the model's game.

## Eight Rules for Learning Games

| # | Rule | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The learning is the mechanic** | The skill being learned is how you play; points aren't bolted onto a drill |
| 2 | **The child owns the goal** | She takes the goal up, or writes it herself; nobody assigns the win |
| 3 | **Scores stay game-sized** | Chosen, temporary, local, and exitable |
| 4 | **Name the proxy** | Every score comes with what it stands for: the streak is the game; Spanish is the point |
| 5 | **Obstacles at the edge** | Difficulty is tuned so the struggle is the pleasure |
| 6 | **A library of selves** | Over time, games cover cooperative and rival, patient and bold, solo and team, across the thirteen lines |
| 7 | **Children write games too** | Designing a game's rules and scoring is itself something to learn |
| 8 | **The AI supplies obstacles, never goals** | Tutors can generate puzzles, play rivals, and run worlds; the aim stays hers |

## Back to the Sidewalk

A few years later, the same two children are in a classroom facing long division, the old kitchen-table misery. This time they've invented a game for it. The whole class races the clock together, with rules the students wrote and a scoring system they argued about for most of a week.

The obstacle is still unnecessary. Any phone in the building could do the division in a millisecond. They chose it anyway. They wrote the constraints, and the struggle is theirs.

In a world where machines can do the math, write the poem, and solve the logistics, protecting a child's humanity comes down, in large part, to protecting her right to struggle on her own terms. A crack in the sidewalk is just a crack, until two eight-year-olds decide it isn't.

## Open Questions

- Can a child learn ironic distance from scores before adolescence, against systems designed to defeat it?
- When does a game designed for learning stop being a game?
- Can the model's own measures avoid capture over years, or only with people watching them?

The first is the one I keep turning over, and it opens onto a harder one. Suppose we succeed. Suppose children grow up with ethical game masters, well-tuned obstacles, game-sized scores, and a fine sense of ironic distance. What happens to their tolerance for the parts of life that will never be a game? Love, grief, and real moral ambiguity come with no rules, no clear goal, no spotter adjusting the difficulty, and no way to know whether you're winning. Perhaps the last unnecessary obstacle a person has to learn to face is the absence of a game altogether. If you have a way of teaching that one, I'd like to hear it.

## The Conference

Picture a parent-teacher conference in late October. You sit on a chair built for a nine-year-old, knees near your chin, across a low table from your daughter Clara's teacher. She's kind, and she's tired; you're her eleventh family since three o'clock. She slides a printout across the table. Clara reads two years above grade level and has already worked her way through most of the class library. Her math fluency sits at the 88th percentile. “She's doing great,” the teacher says. “Honestly, I wish I had twenty of her.” There's a sticker chart for homework turned in on time, and Clara has most of the stickers. Twenty minutes, a signature on a form, and the next family is already waiting in the hall.

Here's what Clara did that same week. She rigged a pulley from a laundry basket, a length of clothesline, and a low branch of the oak, so she could haul her library books up to the tree fort without climbing one-handed. Under the same oak she found a fledgling blue jay, fed it soaked cat food off the tip of a coffee stirrer every two hours for four days, and stood at the fence and cried when it finally flew. She ended a war between her two younger cousins over a single Lego set by inventing a rule with the kitchen timer: ten minutes each, and whoever is waiting picks the next piece. And on Thursday she threw her recorder across the living room because the high notes kept squeaking, and wouldn't pick it up again.

None of it came up at the conference. None of it could have. The form has a box for reading and a box for math, and no box for a pulley, a bird, a truce, or a thrown recorder. The teacher didn't leave them out. The form did.

Most of us grew up on the other side of that little table. The institution in charge of us cared about a sliver of what we were. The rest was ours to grow on our own time, or to let go.

Chapters 2 and 3 described the engine: two systems, Inquiry and Practice, that drive each other when they work on the same object. So what does the engine grow? I call the answer a **whole person**, the breadth Chapter 1 promised.\[1\] Schools never set out to raise one. Their mandate is academic, and nearly everything they measure sits in one corner of one line of development. Ken Wilber's lines of development, widened by Howard Gardner, give the missing goal a map: thirteen capacities that together make up a human being.

## The Goal Schools Never Claimed

### The Honest Statement of Purpose

A school's mission statement is painted in the front hallway, usually beside a mural of children holding hands around a globe. Its real purpose is written somewhere less visible: in its accountability system, in what it's measured on and what it's penalized for failing to produce. I think of an institution's accountability system as its honest statement of purpose.

The adults in the building didn't write that system. Plenty of teachers push against it every day, on their own evenings, with supplies bought out of their own pockets.

In the United States, the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015) requires each state to hold schools accountable on:

- annual tests in reading and language arts and in mathematics, with a measure of growth;
- graduation rates, for high schools;
- progress toward English proficiency for English learners;
- one or more measures of “school quality or student success,” such as chronic absenteeism or school climate.

The law requires the academic indicators to carry much greater weight than the rest. So what counts is two subjects, inside one corner of one line of development, and mostly the part of that corner a bubble sheet can reach. A child can walk into the building having stood up for the new kid on the bus, drawn a heron you'd swear was about to lift off the page, and talked two friends out of a fistfight by the lockers, and none of it appears on the state's spreadsheet. The moral, aesthetic, and interpersonal lines don't have a column. The body and the will don't have one either.

### Whole Child, Half a Map

You may be thinking that schools talk about the whole child constantly. They do, and they mean it. ASCD's Whole Child initiative (2007) asks that each student be healthy, safe, engaged, supported, and challenged. Those are good things to ask for. And yet they describe the greenhouse: the temperature, the water, the light. They say nothing about the shape of the plant. The fifth, challenged, points back to academic challenge and readiness for college and work. None of the five names a part of the person to be grown.

### The Neighbors Who Tried

Some traditions have aimed at the whole person.

- **Waldorf education**, founded by Rudolf Steiner in Stuttgart in 1919, aims at physical, emotional, cognitive, social, and spiritual development. Its map rests on anthroposophy, Steiner's esoteric spiritual system, and owes little to developmental research.
- **Classical education** aimed at a rounded person inside a fixed canon.\[2\]
- **Montessori** attends to the body, the senses, and practical life alongside the intellect.\[2\]

Each names the goal. None has a map of distinct lines with levels, or a way to read how far a given child has grown in each. Any of them would have asked about the blue jay. None of them has a place to write it down.

So the goal falls to whoever holds the child's whole day: families, and the innovative educators who work beside them.\[3\] If your own report cards had a box for reading and none for the rest of you, it falls to you as well. You can't deliberately raise what you can't name. Hence the map.

## The Lines: A Map of Humanness

### Wilber's Psychograph

In Ken Wilber's integral theory, developmental lines are “relatively independent streams or capacities that proceed through levels of development.” In *Integral Psychology* (2000), Wilber gathers about two dozen from the research of others: morals, affects, self-identity, cognition, role-taking, creativity, altruism, empathy, kinesthetic skill, several lines that can be called spiritual, and more. A person can be far along in some lines, in the middle of others, and early in still others, all at once. Wilber's **psychograph** charts that profile, one bar per line.

You've met the profile. The surgeon who can rebuild a shattered knee by feel, and slams a door like a four-year-old when his teenage daughter disagrees with him. The point guard who threads a pass between three defenders, and lies to his coach without blinking. Our culture pays handsomely for a spike in one or two lines and rarely looks at the valleys beside it.

### Gardner's Widening

Howard Gardner's *Frames of Mind* (1983) rejected the idea of intelligence as a single ability measured by IQ. He proposed distinct intelligences, each meeting criteria such as a distinct developmental path, its own core operations, and the existence of prodigies. His list reached eight with the naturalist intelligence in 1995. He wanted to widen what counts as human ability beyond what a test of words and numbers can see. So do I.

### Thirteen Bars

I've combined the two into thirteen lines. Gardner's linguistic and logical-mathematical intelligences fold into the cognitive line. His intrapersonal intelligence separates self-knowledge from the emotional line. Wilber treats the number of lines as open, so this set is a working choice. Someone could draw it with eleven or fifteen.

| Line | What it is | Well grown, it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive** | Understanding, reasoning, language, number | Thinks clearly, explains, builds and tests ideas |
| **Emotional** | Feeling, and handling what one feels | Feels fully without being ruled by it |
| **Interpersonal** | Reading and relating to others | Understands others, cooperates, repairs conflict |
| **Moral** | Sense of right, fairness, and care | Acts on a conscience of their own |
| **Physical** | The body's strength, coordination, and skill | Capable, coordinated, at home in the body |
| **Will** | Choosing, persisting, self-command | Starts, persists, finishes, and can wait |
| **Aesthetic** | Perceiving and making beauty | Notices, prefers, and makes with care |
| **Spiritual** | Meaning, wonder, the largest questions | Asks what life is for and lives by an answer |
| **Values** | What one holds as worth pursuing | Knows what they value and why |
| **Musical** | Rhythm, pitch, and musical form | Hears, keeps, and makes music |
| **Visual-spatial** | Seeing, imagining, and arranging space | Pictures, maps, designs, builds |
| **Naturalistic** | Noticing and ordering the living world | Knows the plants, animals, and patterns around them |
| **Intrapersonal** | Knowing oneself | Knows their own mind, strengths, and limits |

## Where One Line Ends and the Next Begins

A map with blurry borders gets argued over instead of used. Three sets of neighbors are easy to confuse.

### Emotional and Intrapersonal

The **emotional** line is the live experience and handling of feeling: anger, grief, fear, excitement, felt in the moment and ridden without capsizing. The **intrapersonal** line is an accurate model of yourself over time: your strengths and blind spots, your limits, your real motives, the patterns you keep repeating.

One happens in seconds. The other takes years.

They come apart more often than you'd think. You can have a steady temper and almost no self-knowledge: the uncle who doesn't raise his voice even when the Thanksgiving turkey slides off the platter, and has no idea why he keeps lending money to the same kind of friend. You can have deep self-insight and still get hijacked: the woman who can describe her panic, its triggers, its history, and its likely roots with great precision, and whose hands still shake when she stands up to give the toast at her sister's wedding. A fifteen-year-old who realizes, in the car after practice, that she only competes to please her coach has made a leap in the intrapersonal line. She may still sob in the locker room after every lost match. That's the emotional line, and it grows on its own schedule.

### Moral, Values, and Spiritual

These three get lumped together as “what you believe.” Pull them apart and each turns out to ask a different kind of question.

The **moral** line is about obligation and justice between people: what we owe one another, fairness, care, and harm. Its questions have another person in them. Was that fair to him? Did I keep my word? Who gets hurt?

The **values** line is about priorities among good things: what is worth your finite time, money, and devotion. Its questions are trade-offs. Truth or harmony at this dinner table? Adventure or security in this job? The twelve-year-old deciding whether her savings go to a camera or to a trip with her friends is in the values line. Nobody is wronged either way. Something good is given up either way.

The **spiritual** line is about ultimate meaning, awe, and your place in the whole. Its questions have no one to answer to and no budget to balance. Where did the dog go when it died? Why is there anything at all? What is my life for? For many families the spiritual line includes religion, and for others it needs none. Wonder at a night sky belongs to it, and so does facing death.

The three keep one another honest. A strong moral line with a thin values line produces a dutiful person who never asks what he wants his duty to serve. A rich values line with a thin moral line produces someone who knows exactly what she loves and pursues it over other people. A spiritual line grown alone can float free of both: all awe, no obligation.

### Cognitive, Visual-Spatial, and Naturalistic

Why fold Gardner's linguistic and logical-mathematical intelligences into one cognitive line, and keep visual-spatial and naturalistic as bars of their own?

Because schooling already equates thinking with words and numbers. Folding those two together costs the map nothing; they're the two boxes on every report card. It would make a tidier map to fold the other two in as well. I'd rather keep it a little untidy. Separate bars guard against the error schools make every day: missing the child who reasons brilliantly in three dimensions, or in living systems, and has no words for it yet.

You may know this child. She can't explain long division, and she can look at a tangled extension cord and see in one glance which loop to pull. He reads two pages of a novel and drifts off, and he can tell you which of the six bird calls in the backyard is new this week. Folded into “cognitive,” those gifts disappear under a grade. Given their own bars, they show up on the map, and someone can raise them on purpose.

## Inquiry and Practice in Every Line

Chapter 2 ended with an open question, and Chapter 3 left it standing as a hypothesis: does the alternation between the two systems hold in lines where the “procedure” is a habit of attention, as in the moral and aesthetic lines, rather than an algorithm?\[4\]

Let's walk the two systems through all thirteen lines. Every line has a question side and a doing side. The question side is Inquiry: wondering, testing, puzzling something out because it's yours to puzzle. The doing side is Practice: repetition toward a standard, until the capacity runs on its own. What changes from line to line is what gets repeated. In some lines it's a procedure, the kind you could write down as steps. In others it's a habit of attention: where you look, what you notice, what you check before you act. In others it's a habit of regulation: what you do with a surge of feeling or a flagging will.

### Lines of Procedure

In the cognitive, physical, musical, visual-spatial, and naturalistic lines, Practice looks the way you'd expect. There are steps, and they can be repeated: the multiplication facts, the free throw, the scale, the perspective sketch, the field guide. Inquiry looks familiar too. Why does a negative times a negative come out positive? Why does the ball curve? Why does a minor chord sound sad? How do you get a couch through a door that's too narrow? Why do the tomato plants droop every afternoon and recover by evening?\[5\]

The evidence in Chapter 3 covers these lines best, and the alternation runs in them much as it does in fractions.

### Lines of Attention

The aesthetic, moral, spiritual, values, and interpersonal lines are the hard case. Their “procedure” is something you train yourself to notice.

**Aesthetic.** Practice is the trained habit of looking closely, comparing forms, and executing a craft with care: mixing the paint until the green is the green you meant, tuning the ear to hear the flat note, setting the proportion of a window in a drawing, sanding the edge one more time. Educators at Harvard's Project Zero call the first part slow looking, and it can be practiced like a scale. Look at a painting for ten minutes and list what you see in the ninth minute that you missed in the first. Inquiry is the question the practice feeds: why does this arrangement move me, and that one feel dead? Why is the cheap print of the same painting so much less? The habit sharpens the question, and the question tells the habit where to look next.

**Moral.** Inquiry is wrestling with competing claims of fairness or duty. A ten-year-old stands in the hallway in his pajamas and argues that his eight-thirty bedtime is unfair, because his sister got nine o'clock at his age, and he remembers the exact birthday it changed. That's moral inquiry, with real stakes. Practice is the repeated, unglamorous act: keeping a promise when it costs you, serving others first at the table, hearing the other side out before you answer. Aristotle put it plainly more than two thousand years ago: we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts. The repetition builds the habit, and the habit gives the reasoning something to reason about. A child who has kept a costly promise a dozen times reasons about promises differently from one who has only been told they matter.

**Spiritual.** Inquiry is the largest questions, asked seriously: where the dog went, why anything exists, what a life is for. Practice is a habit of reverence and attention, repeated until it shapes how a person meets the world: a moment of silence before a meal, a walk taken specifically to notice what's astonishing, a weekly day kept apart from work. Families give these practices very different content, religious or not. The structure is the same. The practice keeps the questions alive, and the questions keep the practice from going hollow.

**Values.** Inquiry is noticing a trade-off and asking what you actually want: the camera or the trip, the extra hour of sleep or the run before school. Practice is repeated choosing under a real constraint, followed by an honest look back. A child who manages a small allowance for a year, and talks through each month's choices, builds a values line the way a pianist builds a scale. The constraint has to be real. Values chosen with someone else's money, or reversed whenever they hurt, never get practiced.

**Interpersonal.** Inquiry is puzzling out another mind. Why did she say that? What does he want? Why did the game fall apart? Practice is turn-taking, listening to the end of a sentence, apologizing and repairing after a fight, over and over, until it runs without effort.

### Lines of Regulation

We tend to treat the emotional, will, and intrapersonal lines as temperament, something a child is born with: she's moody, he's lazy, she's self-aware. I suggest treating them as skills. Each has a question side and a doing side, and each grows with use.

**Emotional.** Inquiry is naming what a feeling is and what it's telling you. An eight-year-old who can say “I'm not angry at you, I'm angry that I lost” has done something a younger child can't. Naming does work of its own: in a brain-imaging study, Matthew Lieberman and colleagues (2007) found that putting a feeling into words dampened the response of the amygdala, the brain's alarm. Practice is sitting with the sting of a lost chess game without flipping the board, and then sitting down for another game. Repeat it enough and recovery from a loss becomes a possession. John Gottman's research on families found that children whose parents coached them through feelings (noticing them, naming them, and setting limits on what the child did with them) got better at calming themselves.

**Will.** Inquiry is discovering how your own energy, temptation, and focus work: that you can't start homework after a screen, that you work best in the morning, that a goal written down at night is easier to start the next day. Practice is the graded repetition of starting when you don't feel like it, and of waiting when you want to peek. Chapter 10's box on self-control shows how much rides on it. In the Dunedin study, children whose self-control improved over the years did better as adults than their early scores predicted.\[6\]

**Intrapersonal.** Inquiry is asking what you actually think, want, and are good at, and distrusting your first answer. Practice is the habit of noticing and reviewing. Benjamin Franklin, by his own account, kept a small book with a column for each day and a row for each of thirteen virtues, and marked his lapses every night. You don't have to copy Franklin. A child who answers three questions every Sunday night (what went well, what was hard, what surprised me) is practicing the same line.

### The Thirteen Lines on Both Systems

| Line | Inquiry looks like | Practice looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive** | Why a negative times a negative is positive | Math facts until they run on their own |
| **Emotional** | Naming what a feeling is and what it's telling you | Recovering from a loss, again and again, without flipping the board |
| **Interpersonal** | Puzzling out why a friend did what she did | Taking turns, listening to the end, repairing after a fight |
| **Moral** | Arguing whether a rule is fair | Keeping promises that cost something; serving others first |
| **Physical** | Why the ball curves, how to land softer | Drills, reps, and form, until the body knows |
| **Will** | Learning how your own energy and temptation work | Starting when you don't feel like it; waiting when you want to peek |
| **Aesthetic** | Why one arrangement moves you and another feels dead | Slow looking; mixing, tuning, sanding, with care |
| **Spiritual** | Where the dog went; what a life is for | A habit of reverence: silence, wonder, a day kept apart |
| **Values** | What do I actually want, given what it costs? | Repeated choices under a real constraint, reviewed honestly |
| **Musical** | Why a minor chord sounds sad | Scales, rhythms, and passages up to tempo |
| **Visual-spatial** | How to get the couch through the door | Sketching, building, and rotating shapes in the mind |
| **Naturalistic** | Why the tomato plants droop every afternoon | Field identification and the daily notebook |
| **Intrapersonal** | What I actually want and am good at | A habit of noticing and reviewing, kept for years |

### What Stays a Hypothesis

The table is a design claim, and I hold it as one. The evidence that Inquiry and Practice drive each other in an alternating loop comes mostly from mathematics, and Chapter 3 named the reason: testing it elsewhere is hard.\[4\] In the lines of attention and regulation, the “concept” is internal and the “procedure” is a habit, and neither fits on a test.

Still, the loop's mechanism is general. Practice automates a capacity and frees attention for a deeper question. A child who no longer has to fight to keep from flipping the board can start to ask why losing hurts so much. A child who can sit through a disagreement without interrupting can start to hear what the other person actually believes. The model assumes the loop holds in every line, and it reads engagement on both systems to catch the places where it doesn't. Perhaps the moral line breathes on a slower rhythm than fractions do. I'd like to know.

## The Lines Lean on One Another

The lines grow semi-independently. They never act alone. A person meets the world with the whole profile at once, and a spike beside a valley can produce something worse than the valley alone.

### Four Dangerous People

**The manipulator.** A strong interpersonal line, a strong cognitive line, and a stunted moral line. He reads people perfectly and plans well, and he uses both against them. Every gift in his profile makes the missing one more dangerous. This is the con artist, and, in milder forms, the schoolyard ringleader and the charming sibling who always comes out ahead: the bigger slice on his plate, and somebody else in trouble.

**The cynical critic.** A strong cognitive line and a strong aesthetic line beside a weak will. She can walk through a gallery and tell you exactly why every painting on the wall fails. Her own sketchbook holds forty first pages and no second ones, because her drafts fail her standards too, and she lacks the will to push through the ugly middle. Taste without will curdles into contempt.

**The zealot.** Strong moral and values lines beside weak intrapersonal and interpersonal lines. He's deeply committed to a cause and blind to his own shadow: his vanity and his anger, dressed up as principle. He'll lecture a tired waiter about the ethics of the menu and never once look at the waiter's face.

**The fragile prodigy.** A towering cognitive line, sometimes a musical one too, beside thin emotional and intrapersonal lines. The prodigy performs beautifully until the first real failure, or the first question about what it's all for, and then breaks. John Stuart Mill had the most deliberately engineered education on record: Greek at three, Latin at eight, political economy in his early teens. At twenty he asked himself whether achieving every reform he was working for would make him happy, and, as he later wrote, “an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, ‘No!’” He sank into a depression he traced to a training that had cultivated the habit of analysis and nothing to balance it. Chapter 17 tells the story.\[7\]

None of these people is rare. Most of us carry a milder version of one of them (you may already have someone in mind), and a culture that pays for spikes keeps producing more.

### Ceilings and Floors

Some lines set limits for the others.

Wilber argues that the **cognitive** line is necessary for growth in the self-related lines, and not sufficient. A child can't reason about fairness across a whole society before she can hold several perspectives in mind at once. The cognitive line sets a ceiling on what a person can conceive of in any other line.

I'd add a floor. The **will** and **emotional** lines set how much difficulty a learner can tolerate, in any line, before quitting. Chapter 7 argues that the best learning sits at the edge of what a learner can do, where the work is effortful and mistakes are frequent.\[8\] A child with a thin will or a short emotional fuse can't stay at that edge for long, in math or music or anything else. Her other lines grow more slowly than her ability allows, because she leaves every edge as soon as she reaches it. Raise the floor, and every other line gets more room.

### Leave the Spike Alone

So what do you do with an uneven profile? Leave the spike alone. That's where a person's gift lives. The danger lives in the valley next to it. Read the psychograph for neighbors: which lagging line sits next to a soaring one, and what that pairing could produce if it stays as it is. A brilliant, impatient twelve-year-old needs the will line raised more urgently than another year of advanced math. A charming, slippery one needs the moral line tended while charm is still all it is.

## Why Each Line Must Be Raised on Purpose

The old assumption is that growth takes care of itself. Feed a child, send her out the door, and maturity arrives with the birthdays, every line rising together like boats on a tide. It's a comforting picture, and it isn't crazy. Children do grow taller without lessons. And yet the developmental evidence says the rest of a person grows another way.

### Fischer's Web

Kurt Fischer and Todd Rose (2001) describe development as a web of strands: “the strands represent potential skills that are developing in a domain, and the points of connection among the strands represent the integration of these skills.” Strands develop separately before they join, some never join, and each learner “constructs distinct pathways in every important learning domain.” The same learner performs at different levels in different domains and settings, and with or without support.

The web explains the teenager who can give a sophisticated account of climate justice at the dinner table and can't get a single sock into the hamper three feet from his bed. Different strands, at different heights.

Fischer (1943–2020) built **dynamic skill theory** to explain both the consistency and the variability in how people develop. He co-founded Harvard's Mind, Brain, and Education program and was founding editor of the journal *Mind, Brain, and Education* (2007). Thomas Bidell and Michael Mascolo carried the work forward in the *Handbook of Integrative Developmental Science* (2020).

In Fischer's account, a skill is built by coordinating smaller skills into a larger one. Two simple skills, joined, become a new skill that can do what neither could. Joined again with others, the new skill becomes a building block for something larger still. Over a lifetime this compounding carries a strand through four broad tiers.

### Four Tiers, One Strand

Let's follow one strand of the moral line, fairness, through one family.

**Actions.** Lucy is two. Her mother sets a plate of goldfish crackers on the rug between Lucy and her cousin Ada. Lucy hands Ada one and stuffs one in her own mouth, then hands her another and takes another, solemn as a bank teller. Her fairness lives entirely in her hands and the crackers in front of her. Take the plate away and the skill goes with it. At this tier a skill is a coordination of actions with present objects: seeing, reaching, giving, taking. In the first years of life, a baby's gaze, gesture, and goal-directed reach are the raw material later meaning is built from.\[9\]

**Representations.** Lucy is six. She counts out six crackers, splits them three and three between her plate and her little brother's, then drops to her knees to check the plates at eye level. If his pile looks bigger, the whole house hears about it. She can now hold “equal” in her head as a picture and check the world against it, crackers or no crackers. She applies it to turns on the swing and minutes on the tablet, timed on the oven clock. In William Damon's classic studies of children's reasoning about fair shares (1977), strict equality (everyone gets the same) is where many young children stand.

**Abstractions.** Lucy is twelve. A neighbor pays her and her brother twenty dollars to rake his yard. Lucy raked for three hours and has the blisters to show for it. Her brother raked for ten minutes and spent the rest of the afternoon jumping in the piles. She's outraged at an even split. Then, a moment later, uneasy, because her brother is eight and he did try. She's coordinating two representations, equal shares and earned shares, into a general idea: fair isn't always equal. That intangible idea, never visible on a plate, is the mark of the abstract tier, which first appears around ten to twelve.

**Principles.** Lucy's mother is forty-two. She weighs competing accounts of fairness across tax policy, her aging father's care (which of his three children pays for what, and who drives him to his appointments), and a fight on the school board over which neighborhoods get the new buses. She's coordinating whole systems of abstraction into a framework of her own, one she can explain, defend, and revise. Fischer found that this tier emerges in adulthood, and only in some adults.

Within each tier, Fischer traced finer steps: a single skill, then a mapping of two, then a system that coordinates several. A system of systems becomes a single unit of the next tier. That's how the compounding climbs.

The ages are rough guides. Children perform at a higher tier when the task is concrete and familiar. Patricia Kanngiesser and Felix Warneken (2012) found that three- and five-year-olds already gave more stickers to a partner who had worked more, well before the age at which merit was supposed to appear. Change the context and the level changes with it. That's Fischer's central point.

### Dropping Down

The tiers describe a ceiling. Nobody lives at their ceiling.

Watch Lucy's mother in a crowded grocery store at six in the evening, with a hungry two-year-old twisting in the cart seat and six-year-old Lucy asking for candy at every endcap. Lucy points at her brother: the lady at the bakery counter gave him the bigger cookie. The woman who reasons about distributive justice across tax policy snaps both cookies in half, hands each child one piece of each, and says, “There. Equal. Done.” She has dropped from principles to actions: split the cookie, now. Tired, hurried, and unsupported, she does what the moment allows.

Fischer treated this as the normal state of affairs. Skill levels vary with fatigue, stress, emotion, and support, and a period of building followed by a drop is part of how skills grow. Researchers who study skills as they form over minutes and hours, the field Nira Granott and Jim Parziale called microdevelopment, watch learners facing a new problem fall back to a low level and rebuild from there, again and again, before the higher level holds. The drop is part of the climb.

So a child's level in a line has to be read in a setting, and it can change in the next one. And a line grows only where the setting gives it room to work at the top of its reach.

### The Spotter

Most of what children do, Fischer and Rose (1998) write, does “not push the limit of their capacities.” Each learner has a **functional level**, what they manage alone, and a higher **optimal level**, what they reach with support. “Removing the support leads to a natural, rapid drop in the level of understanding.”

Think of a bench press. Alone, you can safely lift 150 pounds. With an experienced spotter standing over you, two fingers under the bar, saying “drive, drive” as your arms start to shake, you get 180. Step the spotter away and you're back to 150 on the next set. The gap between the two numbers is where teaching works.\[10\]

Lucy at twelve reasons about earned shares at her optimal level when her father sits down with her and asks what each of them did. At the dinner table, alone and outraged, she may be back at “equal means equal.” Both are her real level, in two settings.

Optimal levels also rise in spurts, clustered across many domains in the same age periods, and the energy in the EEG shows spurts that “closely parallel” them (Fischer and Rose, 1998). The spurts show up at the optimal level, with support. Everyday performance doesn't automatically follow. A spurt opens new reach in many lines at once, and each line still has to be exercised, with support, before the new reach gets used.

### Untended Lines

Growth in one line doesn't carry another with it. A child can reason far ahead of her age and handle her feelings like someone much younger. A morally serious teenager can be timid in his body. Integral theory makes the same point for the self-related lines: cognitive development is necessary for them, and it isn't enough.

Fischer shows that human capacities grow semi-independently. Wilber's lines say which of them are worth raising.

A line left untended grows by accident, from whatever the child happens to collide with, or it stalls. A school's choice to measure one line is also a choice about the other twelve.

## Initiative and Correction

Every line has a moment when a child first tries to steer in it: to decide, make, judge, or ask something on her own. Chapter 1 met one of these moments in a kitchen. A five-year-old drags a chair to the counter and cracks four eggs into a bowl, shells and all, yolk sliding down the cabinet door, and the first thing she hears is “Get down from there, you're making a mess.”\[11\] She did make a mess. She also made a plan and carried it out.

What the adults do in that moment shapes a line more than almost anything else. Chapter 1 named the result as the second of the book's four criteria, **internal authority**: the adult who comes out the other side keeps a reference point inside, or hands it to whoever holds power in the room.\[1\]

### A Threshold in Every Line

The kitchen is one threshold among many. Each line has its own, wherever a learner makes a first clumsy attempt to steer in that domain, and they arrive at very different ages. A child may meet the physical threshold at two, on a playground ladder; the moral one at ten, arguing about a rule; the musical one at fourteen, writing her first song; the intrapersonal one at fifteen, wondering aloud whether she still wants to compete. Each can end in self-trust or in hesitation, line by line. So a person can come out with a confident body and a hesitant conscience, or a bold eye and a timid voice.

Initiative met with support builds self-trust and an inner reference point in that domain. Initiative met with correction builds a hesitant version of the same capacity, one that looks outward for permission.\[12\]

| Line | The initiative | Met with support | Met with correction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cognitive** | A five-year-old insists the moon is following the car | “Let's watch it at the next turn” | “It only looks that way” |
| **Emotional** | An eight-year-old cries after losing a chess game | The loss is named and sat with: “That one stung” | “It's just a game, stop crying” |
| **Interpersonal** | A nine-year-old tries to settle a fight between younger cousins | The adult watches, then asks afterward what worked | The adult steps in and settles it |
| **Moral** | A ten-year-old objects that a house rule is unfair | He is asked to make his case, and the rule is reconsidered | “Because I said so” |
| **Physical** | A two-year-old climbs the playground ladder | An adult spots from below and lets her find the rungs | “Get down, you'll fall” |
| **Will** | A teenager commits to training for a race | The plan is his; an adult asks what he needs | The schedule is set for him and checked daily |
| **Aesthetic** | A six-year-old paints the sky purple | “Tell me about your sky” | “Skies are blue” |
| **Spiritual** | A seven-year-old asks where the dog went when it died | The question is taken seriously and explored together | The subject is changed |
| **Values** | A twelve-year-old wants to spend her savings on a camera | She's asked what draws her to it, and the choice stays hers | She's told what's worth buying |
| **Musical** | A fourteen-year-old writes her first song | She plays it for the family after dinner | Her chord choices are corrected first |
| **Visual-spatial** | An eleven-year-old rearranges his bedroom furniture | He lives with his layout for a month | The furniture goes back |
| **Naturalistic** | A six-year-old brings home a jar of beetles | Someone helps her find out what they eat | “Get those bugs out of the house” |
| **Intrapersonal** | A fifteen-year-old wonders aloud whether she still wants to compete | She's asked what she's noticed about herself | “You've put in too many years to quit” |

Nearly all of that correction comes from love, worry, or a wish to be efficient. That's why it's so common. Perhaps you can name a line of your own that stalled at eight or fourteen, after a first clumsy try met a well-meant correction.

### Support Isn't Indulgence

You might look at that table and worry. If you're supposed to say “tell me about your sky,” do you never teach technique? Do you praise everything?

No. Support and indulgence part ways over whose intent is in charge.

Bad correction hijacks the learner's intent. “Skies are blue” replaces her question with yours. She meant to paint something, and you've told her she meant to paint something else.

Good support starts by honoring the intent. “Tell me about your sky.” She tells you it's a stormy twilight, the moment before the rain, when the whole street turns violet and the birds go quiet. Now you know what she was after. And when she wants her execution to match her vision, which she often will, Practice comes in on her terms: “Want to see how to blend the purple so the brush marks don't streak?” That's teaching technique, and plenty of it, in the service of her intent. The standard is hers. You've lent her a way to reach it.

Indulgence skips both steps. It neither asks about the intent nor helps with the craft. It just pours on praise, and praise that inflates does its own kind of harm. Eddie Brummelman, Brad Bushman, and their colleagues (2014) had children aged eight to twelve copy a famous painting, then gave them feedback from a supposed professional painter. Some got plain praise (“You made a beautiful painting”). Some got inflated praise (“You made an incredibly beautiful painting”). Then the children chose their next task: an easy drawing or a hard one. Children with low self-esteem who received the inflated praise retreated to the easy task. A standard they couldn't hope to meet again made trying feel dangerous.

So the three moves come in order. Ask what the learner meant. Respond to that. Offer craft when she wants to close the gap between what she meant and what she made.

Her verdict comes before yours. Ask “tell me about it,” or simply wait while she looks at what she made, and let her judge it first. Then your response can confirm her judgment or sharpen it, instead of replacing it. Warmth still has its place. With small children especially, a plain “good job” does more good than harm, as long as it isn't the whole response and doesn't arrive first (Chapter 12 has the evidence). When you want her to learn something, describe what she did.

### Where Correction Belongs

Correction has its place. A child running toward traffic gets grabbed, and nobody asks her opinion first. The trouble comes when adults use emergency moves on non-emergencies. The purple sky is a painting, and the six-year-old isn't filing a weather report. Correct the color and she learns that her own sense of what looks right doesn't count, and she starts painting for the adult. Take over the teenager's training plan and you've lent him your will in place of growing his.

### The Reference-Point Test

How do you tell where a child's reference point sits in a given line?

Watch where her eyes go first. Infants check a parent's face to decide what to do; psychologists call it social referencing. In a classic study by James Sorce, Robert Emde, Joseph Campos, and Mary Klinnert (1985), one-year-olds crawled to the edge of a “visual cliff,” a sheet of glass laid over a checkerboard floor that seemed to drop away beneath it, and looked up at their mothers on the far side. When the mother smiled, most of the babies crawled across. When she wore a face of fear, almost none did. At one, that's exactly right; a baby has no better instrument than a parent's face. At eight, it's worth noticing whether it's still happening, and in which lines.

A child who finishes a painting and looks first at the painting, then at you, is consulting her own eye before yours. A child who looks first at your face, before she's looked at her own work, is checking the room. Other signs point the same way. “Look what happened!” versus “Is this right?” Going on when you leave the room versus stopping. Correcting her own work against a standard she can name versus waiting to be corrected. Chapter 10 collects these as the signs of ownership.\[12\]

A child can pass the test in one line and fail it in another. Plenty of children look straight at their own block towers and straight at a parent's face after a drawing. The pattern tells you which line crossed its threshold well, and which still needs a few rounds of “tell me about it.”

Chapter 1 promised that every line is tracked rather than assumed. The psychograph becomes the running record: where the child's reach sits in each line, where engagement runs high, and where her reference point sits. Some lines read easily, like physical skill or musical pitch. Others, like the spiritual and values lines, show mostly in what a child asks, chooses, and returns to, and have to be read that way. Nobody should give a seven-year-old a multiple-choice test on the meaning of life.

## Cross-Line Depth

### Learning Objects on the Web

Label the strands of Fischer's web with the thirteen lines, and a learning object becomes a point on the web. Its **line depth** is how many strands meet in it. A thin object sits on one strand. A timed sheet of multiplication facts asks something of the cognitive line and nothing of the body, the conscience, or the eye. A rich object sits at a junction, where several lines are asked to grow at once.

The drill asks something of one line. The worm bin asks something of eight, and every strand keeps growing past the junction.

### One Season, Eight Lines

Hana is nine. Her family throws out a bag of food scraps every day: carrot peels, coffee grounds, the crusts nobody eats. She learns from a library book that worms can turn all of it into soil. Over one autumn, with her grandmother's help, she builds and runs a worm bin in the garage.

She drills air holes in two stacked plastic totes and works out where the drainage has to go. She tears newspaper into bedding and keeps it as damp as a wrung-out sponge. She spends thirty dollars of her own savings on a pound of red wigglers, money she'd been saving for a set of paints, and they arrive in the mail in a cloth sack, a knot of worms she can feel moving through the fabric. The bag says a pound of worms can handle about half a pound of scraps a day, so she weighs the family's scraps for a week and finds they make far more than her worms can eat. She asks the neighbors not to send citrus or onions. She learns that a healthy bin smells like a forest floor and a sick one smells like a dumpster, and by the second week she can tell which from the garage door. She feeds the bin twice a week, and she waits three months for the first dark, crumbly castings without digging the worms up to check on them.

In the second month, a heat wave hits and she forgets to move the bin out of the hot garage. Half the worms die. She finds them on a Saturday morning, pale and stiff on top of the bedding where they crawled to escape the heat, and she's quiet the rest of the day. That evening she asks her grandmother whether the dead worms will turn into soil too.

| Line | What the worm bin asks of it |
| --- | --- |
| **Naturalistic** | What worms eat, why they flee the light, how they breathe through their skin |
| **Cognitive** | Weighing scraps, the ratio of food to worms, the bin's moisture |
| **Visual-spatial** | Designing the stacked bins: where the holes go, how the trays nest |
| **Physical** | Drilling, carrying, sorting castings by hand |
| **Will** | Feeding on schedule; three months of waiting without checking |
| **Values** | Thirty dollars of her own savings, and the paints she gave up |
| **Moral** | Living things depending on her; the worms that died when she forgot |
| **Spiritual** | Death becoming soil; the question she asked her grandmother |

One season. No textbook. Eight lines asked to grow. The aesthetic and musical lines run past this object; other objects will cross them.

### Isn't This Just Interdisciplinary?

You may be thinking this is interdisciplinary learning under a new name: study Rome in history, build a catapult in science. Fair enough. The family resemblance is real. But interdisciplinary units join subjects, and they almost always stay inside the cognitive line. History and physics are two flavors of reasoning on one strand. Cross-line depth asks one object to demand something of the body, the will, taste, and conscience as well. The catapult can get there, perhaps, if the child has to haul the timber, wait out the glue, and decide whether it's fair to aim at her brother's fort.

### Touching a Line and Growing It

Fischer insists that skills belong to their contexts. A line grows only where the object genuinely asks something of it. The worm bin builds patience only if the waiting is real and Hana does it. If her grandmother quietly feeds the worms whenever Hana forgets, the will line drops out of the season, and so does most of the moral line: nothing depends on Hana anymore. If her parents pay for the worms, the values line drops out too. Each line's engagement has to be read in the child, whatever the object's label promises.

### Choosing Objects for Their Junctions

The model can now work in two directions. It can start from a line that lags on a child's psychograph and look for rich objects that include it. A child whose interpersonal and physical lines have stalled might build a tree house with the neighbors' kids. Or it can start from an object (a community garden, say) and ask which lines cross in it. Either way, the old single path of one line to one object is gone.

Whitehead gave the rule a century ago, in *The Aims of Education*: let the main ideas in a child's education be “few and important,” and let them be “thrown into every combination possible.” The grid, later in this chapter, makes that rule workable: a few rich objects, opened across many lines and many perspectives.

## Quadrant Depth

Line depth asks how many human capacities an object calls on. A second axis asks from how many sides the object is seen. A person lives among other people and inside systems, and anything worth learning can be looked at from more than one side.

### The Four Quadrants

Wilber crosses two distinctions, inner and outer, individual and collective, and gets four perspectives on anything: “I,” “it,” “we,” and “its.” A brain scan shows what's happening in a head from the outside; it can't tell you what the person feels. A single child acts; she also lives inside a class, a family, and a school. Wilber holds that the four perspectives are complementary and that a complete account needs all of them. Applied to a learning object, each quadrant becomes a different question the learner can ask of it.

Take a question every child meets sooner or later: the whole class is kept in from recess because one child misbehaved.

| Quadrant | What it asks of the object | The class kept in for one child's mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inner individual (“I”)** | What do I feel and think about it? | How it felt to be punished for something I didn't do |
| **Outer individual (“it”)** | What can I observe, test, or do? | Watching what the class does in the week after |
| **Inner collective (“we”)** | What do we believe and value about it? | What the class thinks is fair, and why families disagree |
| **Outer collective (“its”)** | How do systems and institutions handle it? | How schools, sports leagues, and courts treat collective punishment |

### The Same Four for a Teenager

The quadrants open technical and scientific objects just as well. Jonah is fifteen. After a hard summer storm, the creek behind his neighborhood jumps its banks for the first time anyone can remember, and brown water reaches the back steps of three houses, carrying somebody's patio chair and half a fence.

| Quadrant | The creek that flooded |
| --- | --- |
| **Inner individual (“I”)** | His fear and fascination watching the water rise; his assumption, worth testing, about whether one storm proves anything about the climate |
| **Outer individual (“it”)** | Measuring the flow, testing how muddy the water runs, mapping the runoff from the new parking lot upstream |
| **Inner collective (“we”)** | How neighbors upstream and downstream talk about whose fault it is, and who should pay |
| **Outer collective (“its”)** | Zoning rules, flood-insurance maps, and how the town's storm drains were engineered |

A fifteen-year-old can work all four. The flow measurements will take him weeks. The neighbors' argument may take one conversation at the fence for him to see that both sides believe they're the injured party. The flood maps may change what he thinks the parking lot was allowed to do.

### Depth in One Sitting

Your ten-year-old comes home furious about recess. Do you have to cover all four quadrants before dinner? No. One object can be opened in one quadrant or several, and three filters decide how many.

**Richness.** Conceptual, relational, and values-laden objects support real depth. Tying shoelaces or a street-safety rule serves a child better as a single fast loop.

**Live engagement.** Open another quadrant while engagement holds. Close back down when it drops. How do you know how many quadrants to open on a Tuesday morning, and when to stop? You need a live signal, read in the moment, and that signal is the subject of the next chapter.\[13\]

**Safety.** Safety-critical objects stay narrow, whatever their richness.

### Depth Across Returns

A rich question comes back many times over a childhood, and each return can open a quadrant it hasn't visited yet. At six, the recess question may live entirely in feeling: she's angry, and she needs help with the anger. At ten, fairness comes back as a fight about playing time on a soccer team, and the conversation can open the “we”: what does this team think is fair, and why? At fifteen, studying history, she can open the “its” and look at how courts and treaties have handled punishing a group for one member's act.

There's no fixed order across the quadrants, in keeping with the model's refusal of fixed sequences.\[14\] The signal to watch for is **staleness**: a question that keeps landing in the same quadrant across several returns, re-argued as a feeling each time and never tested against what others believe or how a rule actually works. A teenager who answers every rule with “but it feels unfair to me,” month after month, is stuck in the “I.” That's the moment for the adult to open another quadrant. I hear how it feels. Now let's look at why the school wrote the rule, and what happens in schools that don't have it.

Individual quadrants tend to come within reach earlier than collective ones. Young children reach the collective quadrants readily through story, though: a myth about how a village treats a thief is a lesson in the “its.”\[15\]

### Practice and the Quadrants

Quadrant depth serves Inquiry most. A Practice skill lives mainly in the outer-individual quadrant, the body or mind doing it. The other three can be opened around it: what mastery feels like from inside, the culture of the craft, the systems the skill serves. Those are often where worth and ownership enter a drill.\[16\] A piano student grinding through scales is in the “it.” The night she hears a pianist she admires talk about practicing, she's in the “we,” and the scales feel different the next morning.

### Limits

The quadrants come from Wilber's philosophy, and no experiment produced them. Critics such as Jorge Ferrer and Frank Visser have called his wider framework overly hierarchical and culturally biased. They have a point about the wider framework. The model borrows only the two distinctions, as a checklist of perspectives worth opening.

## The Grid

Line depth and quadrant depth are separate axes. Together they give each learning object up to fifty-two cells: thirteen lines by four quadrants. Here is Hana's worm bin across the eight lines it touches.

| Line | Inner individual | Outer individual | Inner collective | Outer collective |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Naturalistic** | Disgust turning into fascination | Feeding, sorting, watching what the worms do | How gardeners and farmers think about soil | How a city composts, or doesn't |
| **Cognitive** | The puzzle of how scraps become soil | Weighing scraps; working out the ratio | Why every farming culture has prized manure | Where the family's trash goes after the curb |
| **Visual-spatial** | Picturing the bin before building it | Drilling holes; nesting the trays | How other families have built theirs | How a commercial composting site is laid out |
| **Physical** | The feel of the castings when they're ready | Drilling, carrying, sorting by hand | Garden work shared across a family | Who does the heavy work in waste systems |
| **Will** | What three months of waiting feels like | Feeding on schedule; not digging to check | Why a family keeps a routine together | The schedules a working farm runs on |
| **Values** | Whether the worms were worth the paints | Spending her savings; choosing what to feed | What her family thinks is worth keeping out of the trash | What a city spends on waste |
| **Moral** | Guilt over the heat wave, and what to do with it | Moving the bin; checking it every day | What people owe the animals they keep | Rules for keeping animals, and who enforces them |
| **Spiritual** | Death becoming soil | Burying the dead worms in the garden | How traditions talk about dust and return | How cultures handle what's left after a life |

The lines the worm bin barely touches have cells too. In the aesthetic line's “we,” the bin opens into the gardens a family or a culture finds beautiful. In the interpersonal line's “it,” it opens into persuading the neighbors to save their scraps.

### Reading the Grid

No season opens all thirty-two cells in that table, let alone fifty-two. The grid is the field of what one object could become. The child's psychograph and live engagement choose the handful that open today, and the rest wait for a later return. Hana's autumn might open four: the weighing, the waiting, the heat wave, and her question about the dead worms. The city's composting program can wait until she's twelve and asks why her school throws its lunch scraps away.

## Navigating the Grid

### Fifty-Two Scores

Each learning object has up to fifty-two cells, and each cell can be run as Inquiry or as Practice. A child meets hundreds of objects a year. The psychograph that should guide the choice among them has thirteen lines, each at its own level, each growing or stalling or going stale in its own quadrant, each with a reference point inside or outside. No parent or teacher can hold that in mind. Try, and you're conducting an orchestra of fifty-two players from fifty-two different scores.

Until now, the whole person could only be approximated. A gifted teacher could do some of it by intuition. Aristocratic families did more of it by hiring a team: a riding master for the body and the will, a music master, a tutor in languages and logic, a chaplain for the moral and spiritual lines, all comparing notes on one child. Very few families could ever afford that.\[17\]

### Why It Becomes Workable Now

The grid is exactly the kind of complexity AI tools can carry. A tool can:

- lay out an object's full grid, every line and quadrant it could open, at almost no cost;
- hold the child's psychograph and notice which lines are lagging or stale;
- propose objects whose junctions include the lines that need attention;
- keep the record of engagement line by line over months and years, on the knowledge and behavior graphs sketched in Chapter 18.\[18\]

The same holds for the kinds of understanding in Chapter 16, and across the whole grid: generating versions stops being the constraint.\[19\] The whole-person goal moves from an aspiration to a design problem.

### What Still Limits It

The binding constraint becomes the child's attention and the adult's presence. So the rule is **generate broad, deliver narrow**. The tool lays out the grid. The child's engagement, read in the moment by an adult in the room, decides which few cells open today.

Some things the tool can't do. It can't be there on the Saturday morning when Hana finds the dead worms, or notice the set of her shoulders as she carries them out to the garden. It can't supply the relationship with a caring adult that Chapter 17 treats as half of what a tutor gives.\[20\] And the observation it would need to read growth off a screen is still largely unbuilt. Cameras and keystroke logs aimed at a child's moral and emotional life raise questions no family should wave through. Whatever gets built has to be designed with the family owning the data, and with an adult entering what the child did.\[18\]

The book's closing chapters take up who runs the grid: a small team of tutors with care and craft, a supernatural tutor that holds the map, and a family that decides.\[21\]

### The Next Conference

Go back to the October conference and change one thing. Beside the reading printout, the little table holds Clara's psychograph. The pulley shows up in the visual-spatial and physical lines. The blue jay shows up in the naturalistic, moral, and will lines. The truce with the kitchen timer shows up in the interpersonal and moral lines. The recorder shows up too, in the emotional and will lines, as a valley worth knowing about before it gets any deeper. Twenty minutes still isn't enough to talk through all of it. It's enough to see it, and to choose where to start.

You sat through conferences like that too, once. Some of your lines are bright because, early on, someone spotted you on the ladder or asked about your purple sky. Others went dark after a well-meant “because I said so.” So pick the line that lags most in your own psychograph, and one rich object you could bring into your week that would ask something of it. Something to do, with your hands and your patience and your conscience. You've read enough about it already.

## Open Questions

- How many lines can one object carry before each line's share becomes too thin to grow it?
- How can the spiritual and values lines be read without turning them into tests?
- Who weighs the lines against each other, and how much of that weighting belongs to a family's own values?

The last one is less a research question than a family's question, and I'd rather leave it there. A family that prizes the spiritual line and one that prizes the physical line will read the same psychograph differently, and I think both are entitled to. What the map can do is make the trade visible, so that a line goes dark by choice and not by accident.

The thirteen lines are a working set. If you'd draw the map differently, I'd like to hear where.
